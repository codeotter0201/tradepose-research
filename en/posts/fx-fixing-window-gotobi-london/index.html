Structural flows at FX fixings: the Gotobi Tokyo fix and the month-end London fix. The cause is known, but is there enough room?

The basic trouble with most technical indicators is that price is an outcome with many causes that cannot be told apart. FX fixings are different: their cause is institutional. Who has to convert currency, at which moment and at which price is fixed in advance, and sometimes so is the direction. This post tests the two most cited fixing events: the Tokyo fix on Japanese corporate settlement days (Gotobi) and the London WM/Reuters fix on the last business day of each month. Each pair runs on the clock of the country where the fix happens; we enter at a fixed time before the fix, read the trade once after the fix and once later in the day, and compare event days with non-event days of the same weekday. The data are Pepperstone USDJPY and EURUSD 15-minute bars from March 2018 to the end of 2025; 2026 onward is held out for validation and was not read. Terms are defined in the research glossary.

Conclusion

The cause is real; the room is not enough. Buying USDJPY from 08:00 to 10:00 Tokyo time on Gotobi days beats the control days by only about 1 pip on average, positive in 7 of 8 years but not significant, visible in 2018–2021 and almost gone in 2022–2025. The fall from 10:00 to 15:00 after the fix is clearer, about 3 pips more than on control days, and is the only reading in the study that survives the multiple-comparison correction; but only 2019 and 2022 are really strong, 2023 goes the other way, and the effect sits at month end and around the 25th, disappearing once those days are removed. At the month-end London fix, “US stocks up this month → sell dollars” is the wrong direction on both EURUSD and USDJPY: the dollar strengthens instead. Moves in those hours are indeed larger than usual, just in the opposite direction to the theory. Splitting trades by the 1-hour SuperTrend direction shows no difference in the Gotobi effect between bullish and bearish states. No candidate leaves enough room to cover costs.

That is the conclusion. Each section below is the evidence for one claim; follow the sections or the evidence links into the strategy report for the data on every parameter set.

Why study fixings: the cause first, then the outcome

Looking at a long green bar and saying “buyers were strong” infers the cause from the outcome. A rise can come from news, a large player, stops being run, or simply no sellers; seeing 10 does not tell you it was 3 + 7. That is the core difficulty of technical indicators: they are all computed from price and cannot tell which cause was behind it.

Some events run in the opposite order: the cause is set by rules or contracts, and whether price responds is a testable outcome. FX fixings are the most cited example because they have three things at once:

  • Who trades is known: companies paying invoices and funds adjusting hedges must trade, whatever the price.
  • When they trade is known: the fixing price is computed at a fixed moment, and the event dates are scheduled.
  • The direction can sometimes be inferred: an importer paying a dollar invoice has to buy dollars.

If all three hold, price should leave a repeatable trace around the fix. This study tests whether that trace is large and stable enough.

How the Gotobi Tokyo fix works

Japanese companies customarily settle invoices on the 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, 25th and 30th of each month and on the last day of the month. These are the Gotobi days; when one falls on a holiday, settlement moves to the previous business day.

Tokyo banks publish their customer exchange rate for the day at 9:55, known as the Tokyo fix. Importers mostly buy the dollars they owe from their bank at this price. On settlement days dollar demand is higher than usual, and the banks know it in advance.

The expected price path:

  1. Banks buy dollars ahead of the fix to hedge, so USDJPY rises before the fix.
  2. After the fix those pre-hedges are no longer needed, and price falls back.

How the month-end London fix works

The WM/Reuters fix is computed at 16:00 London time, and most index funds and asset managers settle their month-end currency hedges and rebalancing at that price.

The expected mechanism: foreign funds hold US stocks and lock the exchange rate with currency hedges. When US stocks rise during the month, dollar assets grow, the hedge falls short, and at month end the funds sell more dollars to top it up. So in theory, when US stocks rose during the month, the dollar weakens into the month-end fix, and the reverse.

Why knowing the cause does not guarantee a profit

Even if the mechanism is exactly right, three problems remain:

  • Everyone knows it: the more public a structure, the more traders position early, so the effect is pulled forward, spread out or flattened.
  • Only one side is visible: we know someone must buy, but not how much, nor whether someone else must sell at the same time. Price follows the net flow.
  • It is small: daily FX turnover is huge, and settlement demand in one session may move price by only a few pips, the same order as the spread.

So the question is not whether the flow exists but whether the room it leaves in price is large and stable enough to stay profitable after costs.

How we tested

  • Time zone: the Gotobi test on USDJPY runs on Tokyo time; the month-end tests on EURUSD and USDJPY run on London time. Daily bars also roll at local midnight, so the volatility unit matches the local trading day.
  • Entries and exits: every weekday enters and exits at fixed times, with exactly the same rules on event and non-event days.
    • Gotobi: buy USDJPY at 08:00 Tokyo, exit once at 10:00 (after the fix) and once at 15:00.
    • Month end: at 14:00 London, set the direction from the month-to-date move of US stocks (NAS100); if stocks are up, sell dollars (buy EURUSD, sell USDJPY); exit once at 16:15 (after the fix) and once at 20:30, which leaves a buffer before the London close.
  • Comparison: event days against non-event days of the same weekday. Fridays have their own behavior, and comparing Gotobi Fridays with all days would mix in a weekday effect.
  • Unit: the 21-day simple mean of the local daily True Range. For USDJPY it is about 0.73 yen (about 73 pips), for EURUSD about 0.0063 (about 63 pips). “Unit” below always means this.
  • Tests: the event-minus-control difference, judged by Welch t and by a permutation test that reassigns event days at random within the same weekday 1,000 times; the readings of one event are corrected for multiple comparisons (Benjamini–Hochberg q); and effects of known size are planted to estimate how small a difference this sample can detect.
  • Pre-registration: event-day definitions, entry and exit times, the comparison and the abandon rules were written down before reading results. The month-end data had been read by an earlier study, so the month-end results are descriptive only.

Result 1: the rise before the Gotobi fix is at most about 1 pip

Buying from 08:00 to 10:00, the 582 Gotobi days beat the control days by 0.015 units on average, about 1.1 pips.

  • Positive in 7 of 8 years, but the permutation p is 0.089, not significant.
  • t is 2.2 in 2018–2021 and only 0.24 in 2022–2025: the effect is fading.
  • This sample detects a 0.03-unit effect (about 2 pips) 84% of the time, so if the pre-fix rise exists it is about 1 pip.

In room, the Gotobi maximum favorable excursion (MFE) median is 0.155 units against 0.129 on control days, and the maximum adverse excursion (MAE) is 0.118 against 0.139. The path is slightly smoother, but the gap is about 2 pips, roughly the spread.

Holding all the way to 15:00 does worse than on control days (excess t −1.38): the small rise before the fix is given back after it.

Result 2: the fall after the fix is there on average, but not stable

Selling from 10:00 to 15:00, Gotobi days beat control days by 0.041 units on average, about 3 pips. t is 2.59, permutation p 0.014, corrected q 0.042: the only reading in the study that survives the multiple-comparison correction.

It is not caused by a few extreme trades: trimming 2.5% from each tail still gives t 2.92, and the median difference is the same 0.044. A bootstrap by year blocks gives a 90% interval of 0.007 to 0.076 units.

Result 3: the month-end London fix brings larger moves, in the opposite direction

“US stocks up this month → sell dollars before the month-end fix” is the wrong direction on both pairs:

In fact, when US stocks rose during the month, the dollar strengthened at month end.

Moves in those hours are indeed larger: the median maximum adverse excursion is 0.38–0.46 units on event days against 0.24–0.29 on ordinary days. The observable fact is “volatility rises around the month-end fix”; the direction is an inference from theory, and the inference was wrong.

These data had been read before, there are only about 12 month ends a year, and power is low (a 0.05-unit effect is detected only 25–34% of the time), so the reversed direction is a description, not a new signal.

Result 4: a SuperTrend direction filter does not help

We took the SuperTrend (ATR 10, multiplier 3) on 1-hour Tokyo bars, read its direction on the last completed bar before entry, split the Gotobi trades into bullish and bearish states and compared the event effect in each. The control shifts the whole state series circularly 1,000 times, so the persistence of trend states cannot create a false effect.

Gotobi days look better in the bullish state (+0.032 units on average to the fix), but non-Gotobi days in the same state also show +0.012. That is the persistence of the trend state itself, present on every day and unrelated to Gotobi. The Gotobi effect points the same way in both states, and the difference is within noise.

What this study taught us

  1. Knowing the cause is not the same as making money. The Gotobi mechanism is real and its direction is right, but price keeps only 1 to 3 pips, and less every year. The more public a structure, the sooner others get there first.
  2. Knowing someone must buy does not reveal the net flow. The month-end fix is the clearest case: the theory says sell dollars, and the dollar strengthened. We only see one side of the demand.
  3. Keep observed facts apart from inferences. Larger month-end volatility is visible in the data; the direction was derived from the mechanism. The first holds, the second does not.
  4. A significant average is not stability. The post-fix fall has t 2.59, but two good years and a few month-end days carry it.
  5. Conditions cut after the fact cannot be used directly. “Trade only month-end Gotobi days” has t 3.09 precisely because it was found after seeing the results.
  6. A filter cannot create an effect that is not there. SuperTrend only sorts the existing trend persistence into groups; the Gotobi effect itself does not change.

Verdict

  • Buy before the Gotobi fix: no. At most about 1 pip, and fading.
  • Sell after the Gotobi fix: watch, do not trade. To pursue it, freeze “Gotobi, sell 10:00 → 15:00” as it is, record month end without filtering on it, and test it on data from 2026 onward. At about 74 trades a year it will take one to two years to read.
  • Sell dollars at the month-end London fix: no. The direction is reversed, and the data have been read.
  • SuperTrend direction filter: not adopted.
Limits of this post:

Only Pepperstone USDJPY and EURUSD were tested, from March 2018 to the end of 2025, and results are gross of spread. Only one set of entry and exit times was tested for each event, with no scan. The month-end data had been read before and are descriptive only. Data from 2026 onward are held out for validation and were not read.

Questions this study answers

Does buying USDJPY before the Gotobi Tokyo fix work?

A little, but too little. From 08:00 to 10:00 it beats non-Gotobi days of the same weekday by only about 1 pip on average, positive in 7 of 8 years but not significant, and almost gone since 2022. This sample rules out effects of about 2 pips or more.

Is the fall after the Gotobi fix a stable signal?

No. It averages about 3 pips, survives the multiple-comparison correction and is not caused by a few extreme values; but only 2019 and 2022 are really strong, 2023 goes the other way, and the effect sits at month end and around the 25th, disappearing once those days are removed.

Can the dollar be traded at the month-end London fix by the month's US stock direction?

In these data the direction is reversed: when US stocks rose during the month, the dollar strengthened at month end rather than weakened. Moves in those hours are larger than usual, but the data had been read before and there are only about 12 a year, so this is descriptive only.

Questions raised along the way

Why can an event with a clear cause still fail to make money?

Because the cause is public, the size is limited and only one side is visible. Everyone knows Gotobi means dollar buying, so some buy early and sell early, spreading the effect out; meanwhile others may have to sell dollars, offsetting the demand. Price reflects only the net flow, and the room left is about the size of the spread.

Why does a trend filter such as SuperTrend add nothing?

A filter only sorts trades into groups. Gotobi days look better in the bullish state because every day is better in the bullish state, not because Gotobi works especially well then. To judge a filter, compare how much event days beat non-event days within the same state, not which state the event days earned more in.

The effect is stronger at month end and around the 25th, so why not trade only those days?

Because that condition was found after seeing the results. Cutting the same data repeatedly always produces a good-looking subset. It can be written down as a new hypothesis and tested on data that arrive after the rules are fixed; the same data cannot prove it.
