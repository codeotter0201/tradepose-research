Research map and method: how a strategy goes from a hypothesis to a frozen portfolio

A common way to research a strategy is to put the signal, filter, entry, stop loss and take profit into one big grid, scan it all at once, and pick the best cell. With many parameters the best cell is usually luck, and a backtest cannot tell luck from an edge unless the study was built to. Our research is built for that: every study writes down its hypothesis and its validation period before reading data, measures how high noise alone can reach, reads the base signal against its own controls, adds entry and exit layers one at a time, and leaves cost, correlation and sizing to the end. This post sets out each stage and the rule it is judged by, then lists the published studies in reading order.

Conclusion

A study starts from one hypothesis with one pre-declared window, a small set of candidates, and a validation period at the end of the data that no selection step reads. Before any candidate is read, the study measures the noise ceiling (how good the best candidate looks on data with no effect), proves with an engine-run strategy that cheats on purpose that its reading can see a real effect, and freezes its thresholds. The base signal then gets a veto list: nine named reasons to doubt it, each with its own control and frozen threshold; disposition follows a rule written before the table is read. Only a base signal that holds gets entry and exit layers, one change per layer, each judged against its parent by whole-backtest drawdown and kept only on a plateau; the filter comes last. Cost, correlation, combination and sizing are judged at the final stage. The set is then frozen, the validation period is read once, and only the record after the freeze says whether a strategy persists.

That is the conclusion. Each section below is the evidence for one claim; follow the sections or the evidence links into the strategy report for the data on every parameter set.

There are two kinds of delayed entry, named from the order’s point of view:

  • Favorable delayed entry: Buy/Sell Limit. For a long, a limit order below the signal price that fills on a pullback.
  • Adverse delayed entry: Buy/Sell Stop. For a long, a stop order above the signal price that fills on a breakout.

What does a study declare before it reads data?

A hypothesis in five lines: the phenomenon and why it should repeat, its anchor in time or state (an open, a close, an expiration day, a profile state), its horizon, the expected size per trade after cost, and the result that would make us drop it. A study that cannot write these five lines is a search, and is read against a search’s noise ceiling.

One base window per hypothesis. The mechanism names its own entry and exit; that window is the hypothesis. Entry times, exit times, stops and hold lengths are not scanned at this stage: scanning them picks the result instead of testing it. Variants of a window come only as later layers, after the base signal holds.

Few candidates, many trades each. The best of N candidates with no edge reaches a t-statistic of about √(2 ln N):

A few dozen reasoned candidates can see effects that a scan of thousands cannot. Adjacent candidates must be different trades: a step of at least a quarter of the hold, and time anchors that are named moments, not every half hour of the day. The trades a candidate needs follow from the design check, about ((ceiling + 0.84) × σ ÷ δ)², with σ the per-trade spread of results and δ the effect worth trading; a candidate set without enough trades is redesigned before it is read.

A validation period. The last stretch of the data is declared before anything is read. No run of the selection stages reaches into it; it is read once, after the freeze.

A disposition rule. Which candidates go forward is the researcher’s rule, written before the veto table is read, and the rule names what it selects for: a mean edge, or control of drawdown compared with holding. A rule written after reading the table is labelled post hoc next to every candidate it lets through.

How do we know the reading can see a real effect?

Calibration runs before any candidate is read, once per family (same instrument, timeframe, holding mode and use):

  • Noise ceiling. The first reading is recomputed on noise versions of the data, 40 or more times each: all trades entered on the same day flip sign together, which removes every calendar effect and every drift while keeping each candidate’s own distribution. The 95th percentile of the family’s best statistic is its noise ceiling. A real best inside the noise band is reported as indistinguishable from noise, with the smallest effect the reading could have seen.
  • Positive control on the engine. A strategy that cheats on purpose, reading the day’s close before it happens on a share of days, is run through the same engine and read with the same reading. A reading that cannot flag it is blind to window effects and is not used.
  • Power at realistic sizes. Entry signals with an information coefficient (IC) of 0.02, 0.05 and 0.10 are planted in turn into each candidate on a background with no edge. The size flagged 80% of the time is the family’s smallest detectable effect; a null result is written as “effects of IC x or more are excluded”, never as “no effect”.
  • Frozen thresholds. Every veto threshold is read from the noise bands here and frozen with a version. A threshold set after seeing the real readings is selection.

How is a base signal read?

The first reading is the window itself: the candidate’s trade t-statistic and mean per hour, against the same trade taken at other entry times on the same days, and against the noise ceiling. A conditional candidate (a weekday, an event, a profile state) is also read against the same window on the non-condition days of the same class; that tells whether the condition adds anything, not whether the window has an edge.

Each candidate then leaves with a veto list. Every row is marked clear, watched, hit or not applicable, with its number and its control:

The method does not write “selected” or “rejected”; the disposition rule does, and the doubts it accepted stay next to the result. Where the hypothesis is a condition, every control is drawn within the condition’s own class (random days within the same weekday for a weekday study), and many hypotheses in one family are held to a false-discovery threshold inside the family.

Two market benchmarks, each for its own question. “The market does it” uses the same days and the same entry time held to the close: it isolates the candidate’s timing. The market benchmark for 22 instruments answers how the candidate compares with simply being in the instrument at the same horizon (day, week or month, long and short), as an engine run in the study’s own unit. Both are runs on the engine, never rebuilt from bars by hand.

Why layers instead of one big scan?

Scanning all parameters at once gives a grid whose size is the product of every parameter’s number of options. The bigger the grid, the easier it is to pick an exceptionally good cell by luck, and the harder the result is to explain: is that cell good because of the entry, the stop, or a lucky combination of both?

In layers, each layer has only one or two research variables, so the grid is small enough to understand at a glance, and you can tell which layer improved the result. When a layer finds no setting better than its parent, we stop at the parent rather than force in another layer for completeness. Layers grow only on a base signal that has held; a stop or a target cannot rescue a signal without an edge, it can only fit that signal’s past.

What rules does each layer follow?

  • One change per layer, compared only with the parent, on the same entries. That keeps the effects of the entry, the exits and the filter apart.
  • Delayed entries count every signal. A delayed entry is judged as intention to treat: a signal that never fills counts as zero, so the child and the parent are compared on the parent’s full set of signals. When no distance passes, the smallest favorable distance is kept rather than the immediate entry.
  • Search ranges come from the parent’s own excursions. A favorable delayed entry runs from one step up to the winners’ MAE Q1; an adverse delayed entry sits around the losers’ G_MFE Q2 to Q3, so most losers never fill. A stop is searched from the MAE Q60 to Q95. A take profit is searched on the MFE and on the G_MFE as separate branches, since they cut different trades. A breakeven trigger sits no lower than the losers’ G_MFE Q3: past that level a trade no longer looks like a loser. A trailing stop is wider than the stop loss and is anchored on the MHL Q2 to Q3.
  • Stops sit outside the winners’ MAE cluster. On the plane of MAE against MFE, the cluster is the dense region around the peak; a stop that cuts into it locks in losses on trades that would have come back, and fires at random as volatility changes.
  • Exits are judged by drawdown, not by earning more. A stop, target, breakeven or trailing stop is kept when the whole-backtest max drawdown falls below its parent’s, with a session bootstrap and a plateau around it. A lower return is the expected price, not a failure.
  • Good parameters form a plateau, not an island. The setting is also re-found when the trading days are resampled, and its neighbours give about the same result; when only one cell is good, it is luck.
  • In exploration the profit side only has to beat the market. While layers are chosen, the average trade only has to beat holding the market over the same window; profit room, cost and correlation wait for the final stage.

Why is the filter a layer, and why last?

A filter decides which days not to trade, and its value lies in whether the days it blocks are bad days. Answering that requires knowing what the day would have produced if traded, which means the entry and exits are already settled. Placed earlier, every later layer picks its parameters on the filtered days, and the effects of the entry and the stop get mixed up with the filter’s.

So the filter is applied to the finished strategy and compared with two things: the same strategy without the filter, and random vetoes of the same number of trades, drawn in blocks as long as the filter’s typical run and matched on the size of the trades it removes. The reading is the standardized mean of the trades kept. Size matching matters because a volatility state removes larger trades by construction, so an unmatched drawdown comparison passes on its own; a filter that only trades less, or happens to skip a bad year, does not beat its random vetoes.

What happens at the final stage?

  • Cost. The net result per trade under the instrument’s high-cost scenario has to stay positive; a target close to the cost is a thin margin even when the gross numbers look clean.
  • Correlation. Strategies are compared on the unit where their risk is settled: the trading day for intraday strategies (the daily loss limit), the week or month for strategies that trade on different weekdays or hold for days. Strategies that move together are treated as one bet; at most three leaves per root are kept.
  • Combination. A combination has to beat its best single member and the market over the same time in the market, on recovery factor and max drawdown, or it is only a larger copy of one strategy.
  • Sizing. Risk per trade is a fixed USD amount: lots follow the stop distance in the study’s unit, without compounding. Selection inflates every in-sample mean, so sizing runs on the in-sample trades with their mean margin cut by at least half (more when the family’s shrinkage is larger). The size is set so that the 95th percentile of the block-bootstrap drawdown of that cut sequence, and a day on which every stop is hit, stay inside the account limits.

What does the freeze decide?

When a set goes to live or paper trading, its definition, its veto tables and the freeze date are recorded, and the set no longer changes; a change is a new candidate with a new freeze date. The validation period is read once, after the freeze, and changes nothing. From then on the record after the freeze is recomputed each month, led by the quantity the disposition rule selected for, and each strategy reads as consistent (inside the band its in-sample trades predict), no market (the market fell, the edge over it is intact) or broken (the edge is below its in-sample band). In-sample readings were computed on the data used to choose; only data after the freeze can say whether a strategy persists.

What unit are results measured in?

Results and distances are in volatility units: each trade’s P&L and every stop or target distance divided by the simple mean of the daily True Range over a fixed window that the study declares. Prices drift over years, so a fixed number of points means something different every year; in units, a stop of 0.8 in one year compares with 0.8 in another. Changing the unit changes every grid and every result, so it is a new study, not a parameter. The session-weighted ATR post covers the unit for intraday horizons.

How is the parent determined?

A parameter set’s place is the last layer it has a setting for: delayed entry only puts it in the delayed-entry layer, with an SL/TP it is in the stop loss/take profit layer, and with none of these it is the root.

The parent is found by three conditions:

  1. Same root: the same signal, direction, and entry and exit times.
  2. Identical settings up to the previous layer: an SL/TP’s parent must have the same delayed entry.
  3. Nothing set from this layer on: an SL/TP’s parent cannot already have an SL/TP.

Skipped layers don’t count: an SL/TP set directly on the immediate entry has the immediate entry as its parent. A parameter set left out of the comparison is never used as a parent, and the report does not look for a substitute. Strategies are matched by their content, not by the run ID, so children from separate scans still find the same parent; the engine computes the entry opportunity once and runs every exit policy on it, so each child’s trades line up with the parent’s (one entry opportunity, many exit policies).

Why compare parameters only with siblings?

Children of the same parent in the same layer are siblings. They differ only in the research variables and share every other setting, so any difference between siblings can only be attributed to the research variables. The parent is not shown among them; it is the benchmark:

  • If children are generally better than the parent, the layer is worth adding.
  • If only one or two children beat the parent and their neighbours are all worse, it is most likely an island.
  • If every child is worse than the parent, stop at the parent.

How do you read this in a strategy report?

  • Stage: research with several layers lets you switch between layers, and each layer’s parameter sets show their difference from the parent.
  • Selection: one click adds the selected parameter set’s parent and neighbouring cells to the comparison.
  • Parameter neighborhood: the heatmap plots only children of the same parent, with this layer’s research variables on the X and Y axes. Colors are centered on the parent: green is better than the parent, red is worse. You can also switch to the median across all parents to check whether the layer’s effect holds in general.
Limits of the method

Every stage before the freeze reads the same history, and every choice made on it flatters the result; children are not independent trials either, since neighbouring settings share most of their trades. Calibration says how high noise can reach, but cannot make a small sample powerful: a null result only excludes effects above the smallest detectable size. The validation period is a single stretch read once. Whether a strategy persists is answered only by its record after the freeze.

Questions this study answers

In what order is a strategy researched?

Design (hypothesis, one base window, candidates, validation period, disposition rule) → calibration against noise → the base signal and its veto list → delayed entry → stop loss/take profit → breakeven and trailing stop → filter → cost, correlation and combination → sizing → freeze, one read of the validation period, and the record after the freeze.

How is a base signal judged?

By its own window first: its t-statistic against the same trade at other entry times and against the noise ceiling measured in calibration. It then carries a veto list of nine named doubts (noise, lone peak, single year, condition adds nothing, the market does it, path worse than chance, thin margin, regime dependence, single instrument), each with its control and a threshold frozen in calibration; a disposition rule written before reading decides what goes forward.

By what rules does each entry and exit layer keep a setting?

One change per layer against its parent on the same entries; delayed entries count unfilled signals as zero; search ranges come from the parent’s MAE, MFE, G_MFE and MHL; stops stay outside the winners’ MAE cluster; exits are kept when the whole-backtest max drawdown falls on a plateau; the profit side only has to beat the market until the final stage.

Which research series are there, and where do I start?

Method, engine and benchmarks first; then the studies of structural flows at known times and of indicator signals and states, in the order listed in the research map.

Questions raised along the way

Why measure the noise ceiling before reading any candidate?

The best of many candidates looks good by chance: with 30 candidates and no edge the best t is about 2.6, with 1,000 about 3.7. Without knowing that height for the actual candidate set, a strong-looking best cell cannot be told from noise; a threshold set after seeing the results is a choice, not a test.

Why is the validation period read only once?

Every reading that feeds a choice becomes part of the selection. Read once after the freeze, the validation period is a check the choices could not have been fitted to; read repeatedly, it becomes one more period to select on.

Why must a stop stay out of the MAE cluster?

Winners’ MAE clusters in a range, and a stop inside it locks in losses on trades that would have come back; a run of those deepens the drawdown. So a stop sits outside the cluster and is judged by whether the drawdown falls, not by whether the return rises.
