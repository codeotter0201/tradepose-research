Research map and layered method: how a strategy grows from its root to a portfolio

A common way to research a strategy is to put the signal, filter, entry, stop loss and take profit into one big grid, scan it all at once, and pick the best cell. With many parameters, the grid becomes too large to understand, and the best cell is usually just luck. Our research works in layers instead: each layer changes one thing on top of the strategy chosen in the layer before. This post does two jobs: it sets out the order of the layers and the rules each layer is judged by, and it lists the articles of our three current research series, so you know where to start and where each article sits in its line of research.

Conclusion

Every strategy starts from a root strategy and then goes through delayed entry, stop loss/take profit, breakeven and trailing stop; the filter comes last, and the finished strategies are then screened together into a portfolio. Each layer changes one thing and is compared only with its parent: children have to beat the parent for the added complexity to be worth it, and when none does, we stop at the parent. Good parameters must form a plateau, not an island; every root and every filter has to beat its own control; each layer is chosen on 2022–2024 and confirmed on 2025–2026. Our research currently runs in three series, Market Profile signals, clock strategies and SuperTrend state, each growing from one root study to a final strategy or portfolio.

That is the conclusion. Each section below is the evidence for one claim; follow the sections or the evidence links into the strategy report for the data on every parameter set.

What are the layers of a strategy?

Each layer answers one question and changes only the settings related to it; everything else is inherited from the parent. For definitions of the terms used here, see the quant strategy research glossary. The order of the layers is the same across the site:

LayerQuestion it answersSettings it changesParent
Root strategyDoes the signal or time window itself have an edge?Signal conditions or entry and exit times, directionNone (top level); compared with its own control instead
Delayed entryCan a different entry price avoid some adverse movement?Favorable or adverse delayed entry and its distanceThe root’s immediate entry
Stop loss/take profitWhere should the stop and the target go?Stop loss (SL), take profit (TP)The chosen delayed entry
BreakevenOnce the trade moves your way, can it avoid turning into a loss?Breakeven stop (BE) trigger and lockThe chosen SL/TP
Trailing stopCan the trade give back less of the gain it already has?Trailing stop (TS) distanceThe chosen breakeven
FilterAre there days the finished strategy should skip entirely?Trend, volatility or Market Profile conditionsThe finished strategy (no filter)
PortfolioWhich strategies can trade together, and at what size?Members, costs, position sizeEach finished strategy

There are two kinds of delayed entry, named from the order’s point of view:

  • Favorable delayed entry: Buy/Sell Limit. For a long, a limit order below the signal price that fills on a pullback.
  • Adverse delayed entry: Buy/Sell Stop. For a long, a stop order above the signal price that fills on a breakout.

Why layers instead of one big scan?

Scanning all parameters at once gives a grid whose size is the product of every parameter’s number of options. The bigger the grid, the easier it is to pick an exceptionally good cell by luck, and the harder the result is to explain: is that cell good because of the entry, the stop, or a lucky combination of both?

In layers, each layer has only one or two research variables, so the grid is small enough to understand at a glance. You can also tell which layer improved the result and which did not help. When a layer finds no setting better than its parent, we stop at the parent rather than force in another layer for completeness.

The cost of layering is that if a layer chooses wrong, the next layer just keeps building on the wrong base. That is why each layer keeps not one single best parameter set but several robust candidates, and one root can carry several paths at once.

What rules does each layer follow?

Every layer applies the same rules rather than picking the best cell by eye:

  • One change per layer, compared only with the parent. That keeps the effects of the entry, the exits and the filter apart.
  • Roots and filters must beat their own control. A root has to beat holding the market over the same window without the signal, or a control sampled from random days; a filter has to beat the circular-shift null: the days the filter blocks are shifted cyclically along the signal days, keeping how many days are blocked and how they cluster while scrambling the timing. A filter that only trades less, or happens to skip a bad year, does not beat its shifted versions.
  • Good parameters form a plateau, not an island. The settings around the chosen one have to be about as good; when only that one cell is good and its neighbours are worse, it is usually luck.
  • Search ranges come from the parent’s own excursions. A favorable delayed entry is placed within the Q1 (25th percentile) of the winners’ Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE), so most winners still fill; an adverse delayed entry is placed around the Q2 to Q3 of the losers’ Global MFE (G_MFE), so most losers never fill. A breakeven trigger sits no lower than the losers’ G_MFE Q3: past that level a trade no longer looks like a loser. A trailing stop has to be wider than the stop loss and is anchored on the Q2 to Q3 of the Maximum High-Low (MHL).
  • Stops sit outside the winners’ MAE cluster. A stop is no tighter than the MAE Q3 and never cuts into the cluster of MAE: cutting into it locks in losses on trades that would have come back.
  • Exits are judged by MAE and drawdown, not by earning more. The job of a stop, target, breakeven or trailing stop is a lower MAE distribution and a shallower drawdown; a lower return is the expected price, not a failure.
  • In the exploration stage, the profit side only has to beat the market. While the layers are chosen, the return only has to beat holding the market over the same window on average per trade, not be the highest; profit room is judged once spread and slippage are added.
  • Correlation is checked only at the portfolio stage. Children are chosen without looking at their correlation with each other; only when every strategy is finished are strategies that move together treated as the same bet and reduced to one.
  • Chosen on 2022–2024, confirmed on 2025–2026. Each layer ranks on the earlier period; a choice stays only if it also holds on the later period and the layer’s effect repeats there (for example, MAE really falls). The earlier layers have seen all the data, so the later period is a holdout, not an out-of-sample test.

Why is the filter a layer, and why last?

The filter is a layer, and it is the last one. A filter decides which days not to trade, and its value lies in whether the days it blocks are bad days. Answering that requires knowing what the day would have produced if traded, which means the strategy’s entry and exits are already settled.

Putting the filter first causes two problems. First, every later layer picks its parameters on the filtered days, so the effects of the delayed entry and the stop get mixed up with the filter’s, and you cannot tell which one improved the result. Second, a filter that helps an immediate entry held to the close may matter little once a stop and a breakeven are in place, because the days it blocks may then only lose a little anyway.

So the filter is applied to the finished strategy and compared with two things: the same strategy without the filter (its parent) and the circular-shift null. It stays only if the trades it blocks are mostly losers and it beats its shifted versions.

How is the parent determined?

A parameter set’s place is the last layer it has a setting for: delayed entry only puts it in the delayed-entry layer, with an SL/TP it is in the stop loss/take profit layer, and with none of these it is the root strategy.

The parent is found by three conditions:

  1. Same root strategy: the same signal, direction, and entry and exit times.
  2. Identical settings up to the previous layer: an SL/TP’s parent must have the same delayed entry.
  3. Nothing set from this layer on: an SL/TP’s parent cannot already have an SL/TP.

Two details:

  • Skipped layers don’t count. If you set an SL/TP directly on the immediate entry (with no delayed entry), the parent is the immediate entry.
  • A parameter set that is not included in the comparison is never used as a parent. In that case the set has no parent, and the report does not look for a substitute.

The same strategy can appear in different backtest runs, for example when favorable and adverse delayed entries are scanned separately. We decide whether two strategies are the same by their content, not by the run ID, so children from separate scans still find the same parent.

Why compare parameters only with siblings?

Children of the same parent in the same layer are siblings. They differ only in the research variables and share every other setting, so any difference between siblings can only be attributed to the research variables.

To judge whether a parameter set is a plateau or an island, look at its siblings: if the entry distance moves one step up or down, is the result about the same? If you put children of different parents on the same chart, differences in signal, direction and anchor time get mixed in, and you can’t see the effect of the research variable itself.

The parent itself is not shown alongside its siblings; it serves as the benchmark:

  • If children are generally better than the parent, the layer is worth adding.
  • If only one or two children beat the parent and their neighbours are all worse, it is most likely an island.
  • If every child is worse than the parent, stop at the parent.

Current research series

Every study on the site is on US100 (the Nasdaq 100 index CFD). Each series starts with a root study; read it in order.

Market Profile signals

Signals from the previous period’s Value Area and where the session opens relative to it, entered at fixed times. The whole series is in volatility mode: each trade’s P&L is expressed in multiples of the daily Average True Range (daily ATR(72), about 60 trading days); the unit is explained in the glossary.

  1. Root strategy: Do Market Profile signals work on both sides of value? 16 events and their mirrors: which hold on both sides of the Value Area and beat entering at the same time without the signal; the ones that hold mostly continue away from value.
  2. Delayed entry: Can a delayed entry cut MAE? Favorable and adverse delayed entries on the 36 setups chosen at the root; 12 improve, 7 with a favorable and 5 with an adverse delayed entry.
  3. Filter: Which SuperTrend filter, in which volatility? A rule fixed in advance picks the filters: longs use the daily SuperTrend in every volatility state, shorts the 1-hour SuperTrend, blocking only when volatility is low.
  4. Filter attribution: Where is the information in a trend filter? Whether the information is in SuperTrend or only in the volatility state; each filter re-picks its delayed entry and is compared with random timing, and SuperTrend × volatility is applied to all, leaving 21 parents.
  5. Stop loss/take profit: Where to put the stop and the target? 11 of the 19 parents find a robust stop, and 3 of them also keep a take profit.
  6. Breakeven: Where should breakeven trigger? 8 of the 11 setups with a stop keep a breakeven; the others’ good results do not form a plateau.
  7. Final confirmation: From signal to final strategy The 11 setups rerun layer by layer in one backtest to show each step’s contribution, whether the filter still helps at the end, and Mondays separately.
  8. Portfolio: Portfolio and position size Three strategies that move with others are dropped, and the remaining 8 go onto a 100k FTMO account with spread and slippage to set the risk per trade.

This series ran in a different order from the layers above: the trend filter was added after the delayed entry and before the stop (the filter and filter-attribution articles), so the SL/TP and breakeven layers build on filtered parents; the final confirmation then compares the last layer with and without the filter. Read the series in its own order.

Clock strategies

No signal at all: enter at a fixed New York time, exit at a fixed time.

  1. Root strategy: Best time to trade US100? 2,070 long and short windows across the whole session: which windows have an edge on their own, and whether it is a plateau or an island; most long windows make money, only a few short ones do.
  2. Clock strategy tree: 14 strategies grown from 2,070 entry/exit windows. In daily ATR units, 15 window plateaus grow through delayed entry, SL/TP, breakeven and filter into 53 strategies, screened down to 14; no breakeven passes anywhere in this tree.
  3. Weekday × window × Market Profile: From 36 roots to a 14-strategy combination. In units of the median daily True Range, roots built by weekday and Market Profile condition grow into 14 strategies, with weekday multipliers set for an FTMO account.
  4. Portfolio performance: US100 weekday clock portfolio. Written for investors: what the portfolio does, its backtest performance, which strategies caused the largest drawdowns, and why each parameter was chosen.

Articles 2 and 3 use different units (multiples of the daily ATR and of the median True Range), so their numbers cannot be compared directly; both units are explained in the glossary.

SuperTrend state

  1. Root strategy: Does US100 fall after SuperTrend turns bearish? One short per bearish period; only a handful of settings have a profit factor barely above 1, so a bearish flip is not a reliable sign that prices will fall.
  2. Full layers: Does the SuperTrend state carry information on US100? The state as a condition for being long: long while bearish on daily and 4-hour bars, long while bullish on 1-hour bars, each a plateau, then delayed entry, stop, take profit and breakeven added layer by layer.

How do you read this in a strategy report?

The strategy report is organized around these relationships:

  • Stage: research with several layers lets you switch between layers, and each layer’s parameter sets show their difference from the parent.
  • Selection: one click adds the selected parameter set’s parent and neighbouring cells to the comparison.
  • Parameter neighborhood: the heatmap plots only children of the same parent, with this layer’s research variables on the X and Y axes. Colors are centered on the parent: green is better than the parent, red is worse. You can also switch to the median across all parents to check whether the layer’s effect holds in general.
Limits of layered research

Each layer uses the same historical data to choose the next layer’s parent. The more layers there are, the more choices are made and the more likely the result is overstated. Children are not independent trials either: neighbouring parameter combinations share most of their trades. Confirming on 2025–2026 removes some settings that only worked in the earlier period, but it is still a single period; each layer grows further only when the evidence is strong enough.

Questions this study answers

In what order is a strategy researched?

Root strategy → delayed entry → stop loss/take profit → breakeven → trailing stop → filter → portfolio. Each layer changes one thing on the strategy chosen in the layer before; a child stays only if it beats its parent, and when none does, we stop at the parent.

By what rules does each layer decide which parameters to keep?

Roots and filters must beat their own control; good parameters form a plateau; exits are judged by lower MAE and drawdown; in exploration the profit side only has to beat the market; each layer is chosen on 2022–2024 and confirmed on 2025–2026. Correlation and profit room wait for the portfolio stage.

Which research series are there, and where do I start?

Three series: Market Profile signals, clock strategies and SuperTrend state. Start each with its root study and follow the order in the current research series section; terms and position sizing are in the method and glossary articles.

Questions raised along the way

Where does the filter go among the layers?

The filter is a layer, the last one, applied to the finished strategy. It decides which days not to trade, and only once the entry and exits are settled can you tell whether the days it blocks are bad days; placed earlier, every later layer’s effect gets mixed up with the filter’s. The Market Profile series did put its filter before the stop, and its order is shown as it ran.

How do children from separate scans find the same parent?

Strategies are matched by their content, not by the run ID; skipped layers don’t count, and a parameter set left out of the comparison is never used as a parent.

Why must a stop stay out of the MAE cluster?

Winners’ MAE clusters in a range, and a stop inside it locks in losses on trades that would have come back; a run of those deepens the drawdown. So a stop sits no tighter than the MAE Q3, outside the cluster, and is judged by whether the MAE distribution and the drawdown fall, not by whether the return rises.
