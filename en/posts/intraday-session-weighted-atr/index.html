Intraday volatility follows a fixed cycle: should ATR be weighted by trading session?

Many strategies set stops, targets and trailing stops as “multiple × ATR”, so distances follow the state of the market. The daily ATR is an average over the whole day, yet the literature established long ago that volatility follows a fixed cycle within the day: a 15-minute bar at the US open can move several times more than one in the Asian night. This post tests one question: does weighting the ATR by trading session bring the unit closer to the volatility the market actually shows?

Conclusion

We split the day into four sessions (Asia, Europe, US, after the US close), estimated a volatility weight for each from 2018–2020 data (US 1.60, Asia 0.69) and tested on NAS100 over 2021–2026. Strategy performance is left out; only the scale itself is judged. Over horizons within a day, “ATR × the mean session weight of the period” describes the realized range with 31–34% less error than the plain ATR, in every year. At 24 hours the mean weight is exactly 1 again and the two are the same. Accordingly, on multi-day SuperTrend trades no weighting (frozen at entry, updated every bar, or a 1-hour ATR) made the maximum adverse excursion (MAE) per unit more consistent; most made it less so. For intraday periods with a known exit time, the main information is the time left: multiplying the unit by √(time left ÷ one day) cuts the error by 36%, and a 30-minute intraday U-shape cuts about 1% more and fixes the bias near the open and the close.

That is the conclusion. Each section below is the evidence for one claim; follow the sections or the evidence links into the strategy report for the data on every parameter set.

The problem: the daily ATR is a whole-day average

Distances in ATR adapt better than fixed points or percentages: wider stops on volatile days, tighter ones on quiet days. But the daily ATR is an average over the whole day, and the same number is applied at any time of day.

The intraday cycle of volatility is a long-established fact:

  • Dacorogna et al. (1993) model the day’s volatility as the sum of the activity of three market centres, East Asia, Europe and America. Accumulating activity over time (their theta time) removes most of the seasonal differences in volatility.
  • Andersen & Bollerslev (1997, 1998) find the intraday cycle in currencies and equity indices alike, tied to the opening and closing of local markets; it has to be removed before volatility can be estimated properly.
  • Within US regular trading hours volatility is U-shaped: highest at the open, lowest at midday, rising into the close (Wood, McInish & Ord 1985).

So the question is direct: if the unit is weighted by session, does it match realized volatility better? If so, exit parameters should be tuned on the weighted scale.

Every ATR here is a simple mean of daily True Range (the day’s high-low range including the previous close), with daily bars cut on the New York calendar date and only completed days used. ATR21 is the mean of the last 21 days.

Test 1: does the weighted scale describe the coming volatility better?

No strategy yet; only the scale.

Method:

  1. Start at every 15-minute bar and take a period ahead, from 15 minutes to 24 hours.
  2. Realized volatility is the period’s range (high minus low, including the close before the start).
  3. Two scales:
    • Plain: ATR21.
    • Weighted: ATR21 × the mean session weight of the bars in the period. The weight covers the whole period, not just its start.
  4. Each scale gets one overall constant, calibrated on the estimation period.
  5. The error is the QLIKE loss; lower is better. Evaluation runs January 2021 to October 2026, with 95% intervals from 2,000 monthly block bootstrap draws.
QLIKE loss = realized ÷ forecast − ln(realized ÷ forecast) − 1. It is 0 when the forecast equals the realized value; it is a standard loss for comparing volatility forecasts and penalizes both over- and under-prediction of scale.

Test 2: a strategy held over several days

Next, the weighted unit on a strategy that can enter in any session: an all-hours SuperTrend 1-hour trend long, entering on the flip up and exiting on the flip down. Three parameter sets together give about 1,600 trades from 2021, with a median holding time of 32 hours; 60% are held longer than 24 hours.

The criterion is consistency: measure each trade’s maximum adverse excursion (MAE) in a unit; the closer its distribution across entry sessions, years and volatility levels, the better one stop multiple fits every case.

MAE (maximum adverse excursion) is the furthest the price moved against the position while it was open; MAE ÷ unit is that distance in units. Stops are often anchored on a quantile of the MAE distribution, such as Q75 or Q90.

No weighting made the distribution more consistent. Among plain daily ATRs there is a difference, though: across years and volatility levels the 21-day ATR is far steadier than the 72-day one (range of MAE Q75 across volatility levels 0.06 against 0.27), because it follows changes in the volatility state faster.

Why it does not help over several days

Over a day or more the weights average back to 1

The weights average to 1 over a whole day by construction. For a period of T hours made of whole days plus r leftover hours:

Mean weight = 1 + (r ÷ T) × (mean weight of those r hours − 1)

Whole days pass every session once and contribute exactly 1; only the leftover part deviates, diluted by the length of the period. Real trades behave this way: for trades held longer than a day, 80% have a mean weight over the holding period between 0.93 and 1.08.

The daily ATR already covers the whole day

Daily TR is already the sum of every session’s movement. For a multi-day period the intraday cycle has been integrated out. The daily ATR changes with which days are more volatile, and that is the dynamic a multi-day hold needs. Session weights only redistribute volatility within a day; they say nothing about which days will be more volatile.

MAE accumulates; the session weight is an intensity

MAE measures the adverse move accumulated since entry, across several sessions. Trades entered in the US or European session reach their largest adverse move in the US session about 70% of the time, and those entered in Asia nearly half the time. The session weight instead describes how much the market moves per unit of time right now. The two work on different time scales:

  • Weight of the entry bar only: assumes the whole trade happens in that session; the correction points the wrong way.
  • Weight of the current bar, updated every bar: an adverse move built up in the US session does not vanish when the Asian session starts, but the stop distance shrinks to 43% of its US value, so the unrealized loss is stopped out in the quiet session. Stop exits move from the US session to Asia, often filled at the open of the bar where the session changes.
  • Short 1-hour ATR: rises and falls with the session by itself, which amounts to session weighting, with the same result.

How a fixed distance is reached over several days

Let the stop sit k daily ATRs away, for any multiple k. Within one day the adverse move cannot exceed that day’s range, so reaching k in one day needs that day’s TR ≥ k. Over several days the move accumulates and no single day has to reach k.

The larger k and the longer the hold, the more often the distance is reached by accumulation, with no day getting there on its own. Yet these paths are rarely quiet every day: the largest single-day TR along them has a median of about 1.4–2.1 ATR, near the 90th percentile of daily TR ÷ ATR.

So large multi-day adverse moves are usually “several days in the same direction plus at least one above-normal day”. The intraday cycle only decides when within a day those moves happen (mostly in the US session); it decides neither how many days accumulate nor which day expands. That is why session weights cannot change whether a multi-day stop is reached.

Intraday periods: time left matters more than the session

The weighted scale is useful where the period is known in advance and shorter than a day, as in clock-window strategies.

If every trade enters and exits at the same times (say 09:30 to 16:00 every day), every trade gets the same weight and weighting only rescales everything; it has no effect. It matters only when entry or exit times vary. The weight then has to be accumulated, not averaged:

Unit = ATR × √(cumulative variance share between entry and planned exit)

  • Variance share: cut the day into small cells; each cell’s share is the average of (TR ÷ daily ATR)², and the shares sum to 1 over the day. For a full day the unit equals the ATR.
  • Square root: a price range grows with the square root of variance, not with time.

The estimated shares: the US session holds 60% of the day’s variance; the first 30 minutes after the open hold 11.8%, each midday half hour about 2.7%, and the last half hour before the close 5.2%.

Test: start at every 15-minute bar from 09:30 to 15:45, end at 16:00, and compare four scales describing the range to the close.

  • Time left is the main information. Unadjusted, later starts are overestimated more and more: realized ÷ forecast falls from 1.68 for a 09:30 start to 0.39 for a 15:30 start. Multiplying by √(time left) flattens it to 0.94–1.15.
  • Four sessions are too coarse. The weight is constant inside the US session, so the result equals the time-left scale.
  • The 30-minute U-shape helps a little. It fixes the underestimate near the open and the close, flattening the ratio to 0.93–1.08.

We also tried the cumulative share for the first 1, 4 and 8 hours after entry of the multi-day trades: the gap in early adverse moves between entry sessions shrank by 30–80%, but the gap between volatility levels grew. The shares are the 2018–2020 average shape, and high- and low-volatility days need not share one intraday shape, so this is not yet a basis for an early stop.

Conclusions

  1. Strategies held over several days: a simple-mean daily ATR frozen at entry is enough; no session weighting. On the length, 21 days is steadier than 72 across years and volatility levels.
  2. Intraday decisions with a period known in advance: use ATR × √(cumulative variance share). Time left comes first, the intraday shape second, and the shape needs cells finer than four sessions.
  3. Judge a scale on the horizon it serves. A session weight says how much the market moves per unit of time over the coming period; it fits decisions over that same horizon. A stop faces the move accumulated since entry, and the two have to match.
Limitations:

Only NAS100 was tested (Pepperstone, 15-minute bars, 2018–2026); the evaluation data was used repeatedly during the study and is not an untouched holdout. The strategy tests use a single all-hours SuperTrend strategy. During the roughly four weeks when European and US daylight saving time differ, Europe opens an hour earlier in New York time; this was not handled separately. Scheduled event days such as CPI and FOMC are another, day-level form of predictable volatility and are not covered here.

Questions this study answers

Does the daily ATR adapt to the fixed intraday cycle?

No, but strategies held over several days do not need it to. The daily ATR is the sum of every session’s movement, and over whole days the session weights average back to 1. For periods within a day the daily ATR underestimates realized volatility in the US session and overestimates it in Asia.

When should ATR be weighted by session?

When the decision’s period is known in advance, is shorter than a day, and differs between trades. Use ATR × √(cumulative variance share of the period), with cells at least fine enough to separate the open, midday and the close.

Questions raised along the way

Why does a per-bar weighted stop move stop exits into the Asian session?

A stop compares the adverse move accumulated since entry. A loss built up in the US session does not disappear when Asia opens, but the stop distance there shrinks to 43% of its US value, so losses that had not hit the stop are stopped out in the quiet session.

How do large multi-day adverse moves happen?

Usually several days in the same direction plus at least one above-normal day; the longer the hold and the larger the distance, the more often no single day reaches it alone. The intraday cycle only decides when within the day it happens.
