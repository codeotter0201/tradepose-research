Position sizing and risk allocation for an FTMO account: how much should each trade of a stopped portfolio risk?

Backtest returns add up one unit of risk per trade; live trading also has to decide how many lots each trade takes. When the account has rules, this is easier to get wrong than picking strategies: an FTMO 100K account may lose at most USD 10,000 in total and USD 5,000 in a day, and touching either once ends it. This post answers three questions: what formula should size a strategy with a hard stop, how should risk be split across several strategies, and how much can each trade risk under the two account limits, and how is that kept up to date? The examples are the 14 strategies kept by the US100 clock strategy tree (2022 to September 2026, 4 points of round-trip cost per trade).

Conclusion

For a strategy with a hard stop, the lot size should follow the stop distance: fix what a stop-out of this trade costs in dollars, then divide by the stop in points. Formulas that estimate the loss from past MAE scale up when a strategy runs smoothly, and a single stop-out then costs several times what was planned. Equal stop-outs are not enough either, because the strategies hit their stops at very different rates and win very different multiples: with equal stop-outs the seven Monday strategies and the four Thursday ones carry 86% of the portfolio’s variance. Treating the strategies of one trading day as a cluster and giving every cluster the same variance (risk parity) spreads the risk evenly over the four trading days; with the multipliers estimated once from the latest year and kept, it earns more than equal stop-outs under the same limits. The multipliers are checked every quarter and changed only when they drift too far. In this example the daily limit binds first with equal stop-outs (the seven Monday longs can stop out together); with the cluster multipliers the total loss (the backtest max drawdown) binds.

That is the conclusion. Each section below is the evidence for one claim; follow the sections or the evidence links into the strategy report for the data on every parameter set.

What limits does an FTMO account set?

Take an FTMO 100K account:

  • Total loss: equity may not fall below USD 90,000, a loss of at most 10,000 from the starting balance.
  • Daily loss: at most USD 5,000 within a day, open losses included. This post uses 4,500 as the cap and keeps 500 as a buffer.
  • The day: the FTMO day starts at 18:00 New York, the same as the research sessions. Every example trade opens and closes within one day, so none carries overnight financing.
  • Contract: US100 pays USD 1 per point per lot, with a minimum of 0.01 lot.

Risk is always fixed and never compounds: profits do not raise the amount per trade, so the backtest max drawdown compares directly with USD 10,000.

Why not size from past MAE?

A common approach estimates how much a trade may lose from the Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE) of the strategy’s recent trades. Our Trades Analyzer does this by default: it takes the 90th percentile of the last 10 trades’ MAE (in multiples of daily volatility), multiplies it by this trade’s entry volatility and by 4, and treats that as the expected loss in points; lots = risk amount ÷ expected loss points.

That suits strategies without a stop, whose loss can only be estimated from past adverse moves. A strategy with a hard stop is different: its largest loss is known at entry, and estimating it from MAE lets the real risk drift. On the 14 clock strategies, at one risk amount a stop-out costs 0.24 to 1.76 times that amount, and the position grows several-fold after ten smooth trades. Each strategy’s first 10 trades also get no order for lack of history.

Each formula gives expected loss points; lots = risk per trade ÷ expected loss points, floored to 0.01 lot. Formulas that use history roll over the strategy’s own past trades only, never mixing strategies, and lag one trade, so no future data enters.

All six formulas stop at the daily limit, and the four built on MAE or loss history stop there with about half the drawdown room unused. They estimate how deep trades usually go, and these strategies’ stops sit beyond the usual MAE. The Monday 18:00→19:00 long, for example, has an MAE 90th percentile of only 0.19 × volatility against a 0.30 stop, so its position is scaled up and one stop-out costs 1,420-2,130 while the risk amounts are only 99-806. Keeping seven simultaneous Monday stop-outs within 4,500 then means shrinking every position. The MAE mean formula has the best daily Sharpe because it puts more size on strategies with shallow usual drawdowns, but that edge does not turn into profit.

If the daily check looks only at the intraday lows the sample actually reached (every trade of the day at its own MAE at once), the history formulas can go much higher: the MAE 90th percentile reaches 571 per trade and earns 238,499 in 5 years, more than the stop distance’s 212,886 under the same check (MAE mean reaches 279 and earns 204,797; average loss 329 and 218,454). But every stop of one day hit together would then lose 7,429 to 8,118, beyond FTMO’s 5,000 daily limit. The sample never had more than 6 stop-outs on one day; with the seven Monday longs held in the same direction at overlapping hours, 7 cannot be treated as impossible.

Equal volatility earns less than the stop distance (recovery factor 19.3 against 21.4). It gives each strategy risk in proportion to its stop, so the Monday 18:30→14:00 longs (stops 0.90 and 0.84) get the most and the Wednesday 18:00→22:30 long (stop 0.16) the least; yet in stop units the tight-stop strategies, the Wednesday long and the Thursday 06:30→16:30 short at 0.38, have the best average trades. Its tail is also heavier: the worst 5% of days lose 1,999 on average (1,956 for the stop distance) and the worst day loses 3,615 (3,365). A bootstrap of daily P&L puts the probability of a 10,000 drawdown from the peak within a year at 12% for equal volatility and 17% for the stop distance, which has more money at risk on a typical trade (a median stop-out of 540 against 399).

The stop distance is used. It fixes every stop-out at the same amount, so the worst day is known before any order is placed, and under both limits it earns the most of the six formulas with the best recovery factor. Its daily Sharpe is slightly below the MAE formulas’, which carry far less money. In a portfolio MAE is most useful for placing the stop and for estimating the intraday equity low; used for position size, it scales up single-trade risk whenever a strategy has a hard stop.

Which binds first, the daily limit or the total loss?

The worst-case day assumes every position of the day stops out together (FTMO counts open losses too), each losing its stop plus the largest overrun past a stop seen in the sample (14%) and the cost. The example holds at most 7 trades in a day (the seven Monday longs), so the worst-case day is about 8.3 times the risk per trade. The total loss is checked two ways: the backtest’s max drawdown, and a block bootstrap of daily P&L (5-day blocks, one simulated year, 5,000 draws) giving the probability of a USD 10,000 drawdown.

Equal stop-outs, uneven risk: how is risk split across strategies?

An equal stop-out only means every stop costs the same. These 14 strategies hit their stop 1% to 46% of the time and their best trades are worth very different multiples of the stop, so the Monday and Thursday clusters carry 44% and 42% of the portfolio’s daily P&L variance and Wednesday 4%; the Thursday 06:30→16:30 short with a favorable delayed entry of 0.02 alone carries about a fifth. So several ways of spreading risk are compared under the same limits; every estimated weight uses only the previous 52 weeks and is re-estimated every 13 weeks.

  • Weighting by performance does worse than not weighting. Recent-Sharpe weights and the equity-curve filter chase past returns, and with only a dozen trades per strategy per quarter those are mostly noise: they add near highs and cut near lows.
  • Strategy risk parity has the best daily Sharpe, at a high price in profit. The Monday 18:00→19:00 long barely moves, so it needs a single stop-out of 1,809 to carry the same risk; the seven Monday stop-outs together then hit the daily cap, every position must shrink, and profit falls by almost a third. With seven of the 14 strategies on Monday, it also leaves half of the risk on that one day.
  • Equal lots per volatility concentrates risk on Monday. The wider a strategy’s stop, the more risk it gets, and the widest stops are Monday longs; the Wednesday long is left with almost none, and its worst 5% of days are the heaviest (2,003 on average).
  • Trading-day cluster risk parity splits risk evenly over Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday. The clusters trade on different days and their daily P&L is nearly uncorrelated, so splitting across the four days is real diversification. Correlation inside a cluster (the seven Monday longs, say) raises that cluster’s volatility and so lowers its multiplier without dropping any strategy. It has the second-best recovery factor and the smallest drawdown after strategy risk parity, but in this re-estimated form it earns less than equal stop-outs: its first quarter has no estimate yet, so every multiplier is 1, and one Monday in February 2022 with seven positions caps its base at USD 545 on the daily limit, leaving about 3,000 of drawdown room unused. With the multipliers fixed (below), the drawdown binds instead and the same allocation earns 224,186.

Trading-day cluster risk parity is used.

How is the cluster multiplier computed?

The stop multiple decides when a trade is wrong; the cluster multiplier decides what being wrong costs. Clusters follow the trading day: strategies of the same day often win and stop out together, so they count as one block; strategies of different days are nearly uncorrelated (the four clusters’ daily P&L correlates at -0.04 to -0.01 pair by pair).

  1. Every trade in stop units: (P&L − cost) ÷ stop in points; a stop-out is about -1. This is also the P&L when every stop-out costs the same.
  2. Each cluster’s trades are summed into a daily series, with 0 on days without a trade.
  3. The covariance of the four daily series over the previous 52 weeks gives the multipliers under which each cluster contributes a quarter of the portfolio’s variance, bounded to 0.5-2 with a mean of 1. With the clusters nearly uncorrelated, the answer is the inverse of each cluster’s standard deviation.

Wednesday is a single strategy, the 18:00→22:30 long, which trades on 111 of the sample’s 243 Wednesdays. Its daily series swings the least, so risk parity gives it the largest multiplier, 1.76: a Wednesday stop-out costs USD 1,382, about three times a Monday or Thursday stop-out, and that one strategy carries the same quarter of the portfolio’s variance as Monday’s seven. The 0.5-2 bound is what limits it; in five quarters the estimate sat at the cap of 2. With nothing else in its cluster, its losing runs are not diluted, and with a stop of only 0.16 × volatility it also needs the most lots of any strategy.

In the backtest the multipliers were re-estimated this way every 13 weeks and applied to the next 13; Monday’s and Thursday’s volatility has run 2 to 6 times the calmest cluster’s, so their multipliers have always been the lowest. A stop-out costs the base times the cluster multiplier; with re-estimation every 13 weeks, the base is the largest value both limits allow, USD 545.

Fixed multipliers earn more than re-estimating every quarter. Most of the gap is the re-estimated schedule’s first quarter: with no estimate yet it runs at multiplier 1, and its seven-position Monday caps the base on the daily limit. With fixed multipliers Monday’s seven positions cost 0.6 of the base each from the start, the drawdown binds, and the base rises by more than 40%. The estimate fixed in January 2023 does as well as the latest one, so quarterly re-estimation adds nothing here. So the multipliers are fixed at the latest estimate (Monday 0.60, Tuesday 1.03, Wednesday 1.76, Thursday 0.60) with a base of USD 785, and the same estimate is still made every quarter:

  • Every cluster’s new estimate within 80%-125% of its setting: nothing changes.
  • Any cluster outside that range: recalibrate the base with the new multipliers (the same 4,500 daily and 10,000 drawdown limits) and reset every strategy’s risk per trade.

Over the backtest’s 18 re-estimates, against the July 2026 settings, 7 left the range, always through Tuesday (up to 1.48 times its setting) and four times also through Wednesday (down to 0.71); Monday and Thursday never did. They came in two runs, 2022 and 2025, so in live use a recalibration should be expected roughly once or twice a year, driven by the Tuesday and Wednesday clusters.

What if the trailing-year multipliers swing too much?

In the clock tree, the Monday and Thursday multipliers are stable and the Tuesday and Wednesday ones move more (0.88-1.52 and 1.24-2.00), yet the fixed latest estimate still earned more than re-estimating, so the plain rule above is kept. Other portfolios swing further. The 15 strategies of US100 clock windows by weekday × Market Profile spread over five trading days, and re-estimated on the trailing year Thursday’s multiplier moved between 0.95 and 1.81. In that case the same method is tightened in three places:

  • Only half of the estimate is used; the other half stays at “every day equal” (a multiplier of 1).
  • A range: no cluster may sit more than 25% above or 20% below average (0.8-1.25).
  • A change threshold: a multiplier changes only when a new estimate moves more than 20% away.

Both account limits are also used only to 80%: a day with every stop hit stays within 80% of 4,500, and the backtest max drawdown within 80% of 10,000. That study set the multipliers on 2022–2024 and ran them on 2025–2026; the tightened design earned more than the history-only rule for about the same drawdown, and its multipliers never changed.

Which max drawdown should be compared with the limit?

The same settings give different max drawdowns depending on how they are measured. For the 14 clock strategies at a base of USD 785 and 4 points of cost:

  • On daily closing equity: 9,999.6, just inside 10,000. The base is calibrated on this measure.
  • Trade by trade in exit order: also 9,999.6. A losing trade can close before a winner of the same day, so an intraday low can be deeper than the close; on this path the deepest point falls at a session close.
  • With every trade of the day at its worst point (MAE) at once, a bound on open losses: 10,743.

FTMO’s total-loss line counts from the starting balance (equity at least 90,000), not from a peak, so a 10,000 backtest drawdown is not a blown account; but once it sits around 10,000 the base has no room to go higher. Calibration names the measure it uses, and live monitoring uses the same one.

Caveats
  • Every figure here comes from a backtest on one sample (2022 to September 2026), and the strategies themselves were chosen on it; the base and the cluster multipliers are upper bounds inferred from the in-sample max drawdown, a longer losing run out of sample is possible, and they are not a guarantee.
  • The fixed multipliers are applied to the same history they were estimated from; only the re-estimated schedule uses past data alone.
  • The Wednesday cluster is a single strategy carrying a quarter of the portfolio’s variance; nothing inside its cluster offsets its losing runs.
  • The bootstrap probabilities resample this sample’s daily P&L and cannot include worse markets outside it.
  • The worst-case day is computed from stop-outs; a gap through a stop loses more than the stop amount.
  • Costs are a fixed round trip in points and ignore wider spreads around news.
  • Every portfolio has its own stop multiples, trading days and correlations; apply the method by re-estimating the multipliers and the base on your own trades, not by copying these numbers.
  • Not investment advice.

Implementation reference

This section converts the settings for the order system; readers can skip it.

Lots. First fix what a stop-out of this trade costs, then work back to lots (US100 pays USD 1 per point per lot):

Lots = 785 × the cluster multiplier ÷ (stop multiple × that day’s daily ATR(72)), floored to 0.01 lot.

The daily ATR(72) is the Average True Range (ATR) over 72 New York daily bars, about 60 trading days, and the unit of every distance in the clock strategy tree; see the research glossary. For the Monday 18:30→14:00 long with a favorable delayed entry of 0.20 at an ATR of 300 points: a stop-out costs 785 × 0.60 = USD 471, the stop is 0.90 × 300 = 270 points, so 471 ÷ 270 = 1.74 lots; a real stop-out loses 1.74 × 270 ≈ 470.

Settings per strategy. The order system always sizes as lots = setting ÷ the expression’s value; every strategy shares one expression, pl.col("entry_volatility") (the daily ATR at entry in points), and only the setting differs per strategy. The expression has no stop multiple, so the stop multiple moves into the setting: setting = 785 × the cluster multiplier ÷ the stop multiple. The setting is only a conversion number, not money at risk; a stop-out costs one of three amounts: 471 (Monday and Thursday), 809 (Tuesday) or 1,382 (Wednesday). The expression reads no trade history, and the cluster multipliers stay out of it (they need the covariance between clusters, while the expression sees only one strategy’s own trades), so the same settings keep working.

In the table, a favorable delayed entry places a limit order on the favorable side of the signal price (a Buy Limit below for longs, a Sell Limit above for shorts) and fills on a pullback; an adverse delayed entry places a stop order on the adverse side (a Buy Stop above for longs, a Sell Stop below for shorts) and fills once price has moved first; the number is the distance, in the same unit as the stop. A take profit is given in the same unit.

Questions this study answers

What formula should size a strategy with a hard stop?

The stop distance: lots = stop-out amount ÷ (stop multiple × volatility at entry in points), so every stop-out costs a fixed amount. Of the six formulas compared on the 14 clock strategies, it earns the most under the 4,500 daily and 10,000 drawdown limits, with the best recovery factor; equal volatility earns 10% less with a heavier tail, and the four formulas built on MAE or loss history earn 44% to 66% less.

How should risk be split across several strategies?

Treat the strategies of one trading day as a cluster and give every cluster the same share of portfolio variance (trading-day cluster risk parity). On the 14 clock strategies it puts a quarter of the risk on each of Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday; with the multipliers fixed at the latest estimate it earns 224,186 in 5 years with a recovery factor of 22.4. With equal stop-outs the Monday and Thursday clusters carry 86% of the risk, and weighting by recent performance does worse than not weighting.

How much can each trade risk under FTMO's two limits?

Compute the worst-case day with every stop of the day hit, then check the backtest max drawdown. This example holds up to 7 trades a day, all on Monday, so with equal stop-outs the daily limit binds first at about USD 525-541 per trade; with cluster risk parity Monday’s stop-outs shrink, the max drawdown binds, and the base is USD 785 times the cluster multiplier (471 on Monday and Thursday, 809 on Tuesday, 1,382 on Wednesday).

Questions raised along the way

Why does the Trades Analyzer's default MAE formula scale risk up?

It assumes the loss can only be estimated from recent adverse moves, which suits strategies without a stop. These strategies’ stops sit beyond their usual MAE, so after smooth trades the position grows and a stop-out costs 0.24 to 1.76 times the risk amount. The research switched to the stop distance and uses MAE only to place the stop.

What does cluster risk parity give a cluster with a single strategy?

The Wednesday cluster is one long whose daily P&L swings the least, so it gets the largest multiplier, 1.76 (bounded at 2): each Wednesday stop-out costs USD 1,382, about three times a Monday or Thursday one, and that one strategy carries a quarter of the portfolio’s variance with nothing in its cluster to offset it.

Must the cluster multipliers be re-estimated every quarter?

No. On the 14 clock strategies, fixed multipliers earned more than re-estimating every 13 weeks, mostly because the re-estimated schedule starts at multiplier 1 and its seven-position Mondays cap the base on the daily limit. The multipliers are fixed at the latest estimate, still estimated every quarter, and recalibrated only when a cluster leaves 80%-125% of its setting, which happened in 7 of 18 backtest re-estimates, always through Tuesday and sometimes Wednesday.

What if the trailing-year multipliers swing a lot?

The 15 weekday × Market Profile strategies are such a case, with Thursday’s multiplier between 0.95 and 1.81. That study uses half of the estimate, a 0.8-1.25 range and a 20% change threshold, and uses both limits only to 80%; out of sample it earned more than the history-only rule for about the same drawdown and never changed its multipliers.

Which max drawdown should be compared with USD 10,000?

For the same settings it is 9,999.6 on daily closing equity, the same trade by trade, and 10,743 with every trade of the day at its MAE at once. The base is calibrated on daily closing equity and the measure is stated; since FTMO’s total-loss line counts from the starting balance, once the backtest drawdown sits around 10,000 the base goes no higher.
