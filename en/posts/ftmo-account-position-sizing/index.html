Position sizing and risk allocation for an FTMO account: how much should each trade of a stopped portfolio risk?

Backtest returns add up one unit of risk per trade; live trading also has to decide how many lots each trade takes. When the account has rules, this is easier to get wrong than picking strategies: an FTMO 100K account may lose at most USD 10,000 in total and USD 5,000 in a day, and touching either once ends it. This post answers three questions: what formula should size a strategy with a hard stop, how should risk be split across several strategies, and how much can each trade risk under the two account limits, and how is that kept up to date? The examples are the 10 strategies kept by the US100 clock strategy tree (2022 to September 2026, 4 points of round-trip cost per trade).

Conclusion

For a strategy with a hard stop, the lot size should follow the stop distance: fix what a stop-out of this trade costs in dollars, then divide by the stop in points. Formulas that estimate the loss from past MAE scale up when a strategy runs smoothly, and a single stop-out then costs several times what was planned. Equal stop-outs are not enough either, because the strategies hit their stops at very different rates and win very different multiples; treating the strategies of one trading day as a cluster and giving every cluster the same variance (risk parity) earns more under the same limits and spreads the risk. The cluster multipliers can be estimated once from the previous year and kept, checked every quarter and changed only when they drift too far. In this example the total loss (the backtest max drawdown) binds first, not the daily limit.

That is the conclusion. Each section below is the evidence for one claim; follow the sections or the evidence links into the strategy report for the data on every parameter set.

What limits does an FTMO account set?

Take an FTMO 100K account:

  • Total loss: equity may not fall below USD 90,000, a loss of at most 10,000 from the starting balance.
  • Daily loss: at most USD 5,000 within a day, open losses included. This post uses 4,500 as the cap and keeps 500 as a buffer.
  • The day: the FTMO day starts at 18:00 New York, the same as the research sessions. Every example trade opens and closes within one day, so none carries overnight financing.
  • Contract: US100 pays USD 1 per point per lot, with a minimum of 0.01 lot.

Risk is always fixed and never compounds: profits do not raise the amount per trade, so the backtest max drawdown compares directly with USD 10,000.

Why not size from past MAE?

A common approach estimates how much a trade may lose from the Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE) of the strategy’s recent trades. Our Trades Analyzer does this by default: it takes the 90th percentile of the last 10 trades’ MAE (in multiples of daily volatility), multiplies it by this trade’s entry volatility and by 4, and treats that as the expected loss in points; lots = risk amount ÷ expected loss points.

That suits strategies without a stop, whose loss can only be estimated from past adverse moves. A strategy with a hard stop is different: its largest loss is known at entry, and estimating it from MAE lets the real risk drift. On the 10 clock strategies, at one risk amount a stop-out costs 0.24 to 1.75 times that amount, and the position grows several-fold after ten smooth trades. Each strategy’s first 10 trades also get no order for lack of history.

The four formulas built on MAE or loss history all stop at the daily limit with drawdown room left. They estimate how deep trades usually go, and these strategies’ stops sit beyond the usual MAE. The Monday 18:00→19:00 long, for example, has an MAE 90th percentile of only 0.19 × volatility against a 0.30 stop, so its position is scaled up and one stop-out costs 2,300-3,500. Keeping four simultaneous stop-outs within 4,500 then means shrinking every position. The MAE mean formula has the best daily Sharpe because it puts more size on strategies with shallow usual drawdowns, but that edge does not turn into profit.

If the daily check looks only at the intraday lows the sample actually reached (every trade of the day at its own MAE at once), MAE mean can go up to 306 per trade and earn 172,623 in 5 years, about as much as the stop distance; but four stop-outs on one day would then lose 6,470, beyond FTMO’s 5,000 daily limit. The sample never had more than 3 stop-outs on one day; with the four Monday longs held in the same direction at the same hours, 4 cannot be treated as impossible.

Equal volatility earns the most (recovery factor 20.1), because the wider a strategy’s stop, the more risk it gets, and the widest stops belong to the Monday longs, the best performers in-sample. The price is in the tail: the worst 5% of days lose 2,186 on average (1,649 for the stop distance), the worst day loses 3,094, and a bootstrap of daily P&L puts the probability of a 10,000 drawdown from the peak within a year at 17% (9% for the stop distance). It amounts to sizing up on in-sample performance, with no reason known in advance.

The stop distance is used. It fixes every stop-out at the same amount, so the worst day is known before any order is placed; its daily Sharpe matches the others, and under both limits only equal volatility earns more. In a portfolio MAE is most useful for placing the stop and for estimating the intraday equity low; used for position size, it scales up single-trade risk whenever a strategy has a hard stop.

Which binds first, the daily limit or the total loss?

The worst-case day assumes every position of the day stops out together (FTMO counts open losses too, and no position can lose more than its stop). The example holds at most 4 trades in a day (the four Monday longs), so the worst-case day is about 4 times the risk per trade. The total loss is checked two ways: the backtest’s max drawdown, and a block bootstrap of daily P&L (5-day blocks, one simulated year, 5,000 draws) giving the probability of a USD 10,000 drawdown.

Equal stop-outs, uneven risk: how is risk split across strategies?

An equal stop-out only means every stop costs the same. These 10 strategies hit their stop 1% to 45% of the time and their best trades are worth very different multiples of the stop, so the Thursday cluster carries 60% of the portfolio’s daily P&L variance, a third of it from the Thursday 06:30→16:00 short alone. So several ways of spreading risk are compared under the same limits; every estimated weight uses only the previous 52 weeks and is re-estimated every 13 weeks.

  • Weighting by performance does worse than not weighting. Recent-Sharpe weights and the equity-curve filter chase past returns, and with only a dozen trades per strategy per quarter those are mostly noise: they add near highs and cut near lows.
  • Strategy risk parity spreads risk most evenly, at a high price. The Monday 18:00→19:00 long barely moves, so it needs a single stop-out of 2,559 to carry the same risk; four Monday stop-outs together then hit the daily cap, every position must shrink, and profit falls by a fifth.
  • Equal lots per volatility earns the most but concentrates risk. The wider a strategy’s stop, the more risk it gets, which happens to be the Monday longs, the best in-sample, and its worst 5% of days are the heaviest (2,213 on average).
  • Trading-day cluster risk parity splits risk evenly over Monday, Tuesday and Thursday. The clusters trade on different days and their daily P&L is nearly uncorrelated, so splitting across the three days is real diversification. Correlation inside a cluster (the four Monday longs, say) raises that cluster’s volatility and so lowers its multiplier without dropping any strategy; dropping the strategies correlated above 0.4 actually stretched the time to double from 29 to 37 months.

Trading-day cluster risk parity is used.

How is the cluster multiplier computed?

The stop multiple decides when a trade is wrong; the cluster multiplier decides what being wrong costs. Clusters follow the trading day: strategies of the same day often win and stop out together, so they count as one block; strategies of different days are nearly uncorrelated (the three clusters’ daily P&L correlates at about -0.03).

  1. Every trade in stop units: (P&L − cost) ÷ stop in points; a stop-out is about -1. This is also the P&L when every stop-out costs the same.
  2. Each cluster’s trades are summed into a daily series, with 0 on days without a trade.
  3. The covariance of the three daily series over the previous 52 weeks gives the multipliers under which each cluster contributes a third of the portfolio’s variance, bounded to 0.5-2 with a mean of 1. With the clusters nearly uncorrelated, the answer is the inverse of each cluster’s standard deviation.

In the backtest the multipliers were re-estimated this way every 13 weeks and applied to the next 13; Thursday’s volatility has run 1.6 to 2 times the others’, so its multiplier has always been the lowest. Must the cluster multipliers be re-estimated every quarter?

The three multipliers have been stable, so estimating them once and keeping them was tested too:

Fixed multipliers do as well as re-estimating every quarter, which mostly chases noise. So the multipliers are fixed at the latest estimate (Monday 1.18, Tuesday 1.12, Thursday 0.70) with a base of USD 743, and the same estimate is still made every quarter:

  • Every cluster’s new estimate within 80%-125% of its setting: nothing changes.
  • Any cluster outside that range: recalibrate the base with the new multipliers (the same 4,500 daily and 10,000 drawdown limits) and reset every strategy’s risk per trade.

Over the backtest’s 18 re-estimates, against the July 2026 settings, the range was left only once: in July 2025, Tuesday’s new estimate was 1.26 times its setting.

Sizing each strategy by the average swing of its own last 52 trades was tested as well. It needs no manual update, but it cannot see the correlation between strategies and it scales up the Monday 18:00→19:00 long, which barely moves; it earns only 138,841 in 5 years with a recovery factor of 13.9 and takes 42 months to double, so it is not used.

What if the trailing-year multipliers swing too much?

The clock tree’s three multipliers are stable, but not every portfolio’s are. The 15 strategies of US100 clock windows by weekday × Market Profile spread over five trading days, and re-estimated on the trailing year Thursday’s multiplier moved between 0.95 and 1.81. In that case the same method is tightened in three places:

  • Only half of the estimate is used; the other half stays at “every day equal” (a multiplier of 1).
  • A range: no cluster may sit more than 25% above or 20% below average (0.8-1.25).
  • A change threshold: a multiplier changes only when a new estimate moves more than 20% away.

Both account limits are also used only to 80%: a day with every stop hit stays within 80% of 4,500, and the backtest max drawdown within 80% of 10,000. That study set the multipliers on 2022–2024 and ran them on 2025–2026; the tightened design earned more than the history-only rule for about the same drawdown, and its multipliers never changed.

Which max drawdown should be compared with the limit?

The same settings give max drawdowns hundreds of dollars apart depending on how they are measured. For the 10 clock strategies at a base of USD 743 and 4 points of cost:

  • On daily closing equity: 9,986, inside 10,000. The base is calibrated on this measure.
  • Trade by trade in exit order: 10,578. A losing trade can close before a winner of the same day, so the intraday low is deeper than the close.
  • With open losses included: 10,923.

FTMO’s total-loss line counts from the starting balance (equity at least 90,000), not from a peak, so a 10,000 backtest drawdown is not a blown account; but once it sits around 10,000 the base has no room to go higher. Calibration names the measure it uses, and live monitoring uses the same one.

Caveats
  • Every figure here comes from a backtest on one sample (2022 to September 2026), and the strategies themselves were chosen on it; the base and the cluster multipliers are upper bounds inferred from the in-sample max drawdown, a longer losing run out of sample is possible, and they are not a guarantee.
  • The bootstrap probabilities resample this sample’s daily P&L and cannot include worse markets outside it.
  • The worst-case day is computed from stop-outs; a gap through a stop loses more than the stop amount.
  • Costs are a fixed round trip in points and ignore wider spreads around news.
  • Every portfolio has its own stop multiples, trading days and correlations; apply the method by re-estimating the multipliers and the base on your own trades, not by copying these numbers.
  • Not investment advice.

Implementation reference

This section converts the settings for the order system; readers can skip it.

Lots. First fix what a stop-out of this trade costs, then work back to lots (US100 pays USD 1 per point per lot):

Lots = 743 × the cluster multiplier ÷ (stop multiple × that day’s daily ATR(72)), floored to 0.01 lot.

The daily ATR(72) is the Average True Range (ATR) over 72 New York daily bars, about 60 trading days, and the unit of every distance in the clock strategy tree; see the research glossary. For the Monday 18:00→14:00 long at an ATR of 300 points: a stop-out costs 743 × 1.18 = USD 877, the stop is 0.68 × 300 = 204 points, so 877 ÷ 204 = 4.29 lots; a real stop-out loses 4.29 × 204 ≈ 875.

Settings per strategy. The order system always sizes as lots = setting ÷ the expression’s value; every strategy shares one expression, pl.col("entry_volatility") (the daily ATR at entry in points), and only the setting differs per strategy. The expression has no stop multiple, so the stop multiple moves into the setting: setting = 743 × the cluster multiplier ÷ the stop multiple. The setting is only a conversion number, not money at risk; a stop-out costs one of three amounts: 877, 832 or 520. The expression reads no trade history, and the cluster multipliers stay out of it (they need the covariance between clusters, while the expression sees only one strategy’s own trades), so the same settings keep working.

In the table, a favorable delayed entry places a limit order on the favorable side of the signal price (a Buy Limit below for longs, a Sell Limit above for shorts) and fills on a pullback; an adverse delayed entry places a stop order on the adverse side (a Buy Stop above for longs, a Sell Stop below for shorts) and fills once price has moved first; the number is the distance, in the same unit as the stop.

Questions this study answers

What formula should size a strategy with a hard stop?

The stop distance: lots = stop-out amount ÷ (stop multiple × volatility at entry in points), so every stop-out costs a fixed amount. Of the six formulas compared on the 10 clock strategies, only equal volatility earns more under the 4,500 daily and 10,000 drawdown limits, and equal volatility amounts to sizing up on in-sample performance with a heavier tail; the four formulas built on MAE or loss history all stop at the daily limit and earn 20% to 60% less.

How should risk be split across several strategies?

Treat the strategies of one trading day as a cluster and give every cluster the same share of portfolio variance (trading-day cluster risk parity). On the 10 clock strategies it puts a third of the risk on each of Monday, Tuesday and Thursday and earns 197,717 in 5 years with a recovery factor of 19.8; with equal stop-outs the Thursday cluster alone carries 60% of the risk, and weighting by recent performance does worse than not weighting.

How much can each trade risk under FTMO's two limits?

Compute the worst-case day with every stop of the day hit, then check the backtest max drawdown. This example holds at most 4 trades a day, far from 4,500, so the max drawdown binds first: with equal stop-outs the cap is about USD 625 per trade, and with cluster risk parity the base is USD 743 times the cluster multiplier (877 on Monday, 832 on Tuesday, 520 on Thursday).

Questions raised along the way

Why does the Trades Analyzer's default MAE formula scale risk up?

It assumes the loss can only be estimated from recent adverse moves, which suits strategies without a stop. These strategies’ stops sit beyond their usual MAE, so after smooth trades the position grows and a stop-out costs 0.24 to 1.75 times the risk amount. The research switched to the stop distance and uses MAE only to place the stop.

Must the cluster multipliers be re-estimated every quarter?

No. On the 10 clock strategies, re-estimating every 13 weeks and estimating once give almost the same 5-year profit and recovery factor; re-estimation mostly chases noise. The multipliers are fixed at the latest estimate, still estimated every quarter, and recalibrated only when a cluster leaves 80%-125% of its setting, which happened once in 18 backtest re-estimates.

What if the trailing-year multipliers swing a lot?

The 15 weekday × Market Profile strategies are such a case, with Thursday’s multiplier between 0.95 and 1.81. That study uses half of the estimate, a 0.8-1.25 range and a 20% change threshold, and uses both limits only to 80%; out of sample it earned more than the history-only rule for about the same drawdown and never changed its multipliers.

Which max drawdown should be compared with USD 10,000?

For the same settings it is 9,986 on daily closing equity, 10,578 trade by trade and 10,923 with open losses. The base is calibrated on daily closing equity and the measure is stated; since FTMO’s total-loss line counts from the starting balance, once the backtest drawdown sits around 10,000 the base goes no higher.
