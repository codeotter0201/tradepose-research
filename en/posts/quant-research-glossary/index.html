MAE, G_MFE and profit factor: a quant research glossary

It also collects the methods our studies share, such as controls, the choice of volatility unit, and how take profits and filters are judged, and lists the Market Profile signal codes. Conclusion

Before reading our research, there are three things you most need to know. First, MFE only measures the favorable move before the Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE) occurs; the largest favorable move over the whole holding period is called G_MFE, which differs from the MFE in most textbooks. Second, returns, drawdowns and MAE come in three units: percentage (%), multiples of the daily ATR, and multiples of the median True Range, and numbers in different units do not compare directly. Third, there are only two kinds of delayed entry: favorable delayed entry (Buy/Sell Limit: a long rests a limit order below the signal price and waits for a pullback) and adverse delayed entry (Buy/Sell Stop: a long rests a stop order above the signal price and waits for a breakout). Favorable and adverse are named from the order’s point of view; once the position is open, a move in the same direction gets the opposite name.

That is the conclusion. Each section below is the evidence for one claim; follow the sections or the evidence links into the strategy report for the data on every parameter set.

What units are returns and drawdowns in?

The units of returns, drawdowns, per-trade P&L, MAE and similar values are set by the calculation mode (Analyzer mode); win rate, ratios and day counts are not affected. When an article offers two or more modes, you can switch between them in the navigation bar.

Calculation modeHow it is calculatedUnit
PercentageEach trade’s P&L ÷ entry price, then summed%
Volatility-normalizedEach trade’s P&L ÷ volatility at exit, then summed; equivalent to every trade taking the same volatility riskvol (multiples of volatility)
AbsolutePrice points per unit; in US dollars once position size is appliedpts

In every mode, total return is a simple sum: each trade’s result is added directly, ignoring position size and compounding.

Volatility-normalized mode needs a volatility series as its unit. Our research has used two, so returns in our articles actually come in three units:

UnitEach trade’s P&L divided byUsed in
PercentageEntry priceSwitchable in every article; not the main unit of comparison
Multiples of the daily ATRThe New York daily ATR(72) at exit, about 60 trading daysMarket Profile signals, SuperTrend state, the clock root and the first clock strategy tree
Multiples of the median True RangeThe median True Range of the previous 143 New York daily bars at exitThe weekday × Market Profile clock combination and its portfolio

Numbers in the three units do not compare directly:

  • There is no fixed conversion between percentage and volatility multiples. Volatility relative to price changes every year: the Nasdaq’s daily point range at 30,000 is far larger than at 15,000, and calm and volatile years have different ratios. Converted to percentage, the same trades give more weight to volatile periods, which changes which parameter sets look best.
  • The two volatility multiples have different scales. The median True Range of 143 daily bars is about 0.92 times the daily ATR(72), so the same “0.5 ×” stands for a slightly shorter distance in the median version.
  • The samples differ too. The median version’s indicators warm up until June 2022, so its statistics start on June 20, 2022; the daily ATR version starts in late January 2022.

So before comparing numbers from two articles, check their unit and sample period (listed under “Method and data” at the end of each article).

The same volatility series is sampled at different times for different purposes:

PurposeWhich volatility is used
Per-trade P&L in volatility-normalized modeAt exit (exit volatility)
Stop loss, take profit and delayed entry distancesAt entry (entry volatility)
MAE, G_MFE and MFE in volatility-normalized modeWhen the excursion occurs (event-time volatility)

Intraday strategies enter and exit on the same day, so the daily volatility is the same at all three times. Strategies that hold across days fix the volatility at the entry signal and keep it for the whole trade, so exit and event-time volatility equal the entry volatility and are not distorted by how volatility changed during the trade.

For example, a long that makes 2 points with an entry price of 100 and exit volatility of 40 points is +2% in percentage mode, +0.05 vol in volatility-normalized mode and +2 pts in absolute mode.

Why a daily ATR over about 60 trading days?

Volatility is the unit shared by P&L, stop loss, take profit and delayed entry. Average True Range (ATR) is the average true range over a period, written as “bar frequency + ATR(bars)”, for example daily ATR(72) or 1-hour ATR(460). We choose the volatility unit by three requirements: the same unit as stops and targets, known before the trade, and the same for every trade on a given day.

  • ATR rather than the standard deviation of returns: ATR is in price points, so it can be written directly as “stop loss 0.5 × ATR”, and the true range includes gaps, so overnight gaps count as volatility.
  • Daily rather than 15-minute: a 15-minute ATR changes within the day with the session, quiet before the open and wild after it, so the unit would depend on the entry time. The daily ATR is taken from the previous completed daily bar: every trade that day uses the same value, known before the open, and a resting order’s price stays fixed from the signal to the exit.
  • About 60 trading days: too short, and one volatile week swings the unit; too long, and it cannot follow the move from the 2022 bear market to the calmer years after it.

We measured which ruler is most stable. On 26 US100 Market Profile setups (late January 2022 to September 23, 2026, Tuesday to Friday, immediate entry), we computed each setup’s 90th percentile of MAE year by year and looked at its coefficient of variation across years: the smaller it is, the more consistent MAE is from year to year when measured with that ruler.

ScaleCoefficient of variation of the yearly MAE 90th percentile (median of 26 setups)
Daily ATR, about 60 trading days0.123
4-hour ATR, about 60 trading days0.132
1-hour ATR, about 60 trading days0.133
Daily ATR, about 20 trading days0.164
15-minute ATR (96 bars, about 1 day)0.179
Points0.233
Percent of price0.298

Lookback length matters more than bar timeframe: 60-day daily, 4-hour and 1-hour ATR are about the same, while 20 days is clearly worse; fixed points and percent of price are the least stable. With a daily ATR of about 20, 40 or 120 trading days instead, the rankings and first choices of the Market Profile signals also stay largely the same; see Do Market Profile signals work on both sides of value?.

Daily ATR(72) averages 72 New York daily bars. The New York daily series counts Sunday evening’s short session as its own bar, about one bar in six, so 72 bars is about 60 trading days. That bar’s range is only about a third of a weekday’s, so ATR(72) is about 10% below an ATR of weekdays only. The offset is steady and does not change rankings, but keep it in mind when converting to points: “0.5 × daily ATR(72)” is about 0.45 times the weekday volatility.

Why do some studies use a median True Range?

So that a bar’s volatility is the same no matter where the calculation starts. The Wilder ATR is a recursive average whose every value carries the start of its window: move the start and every later value differs a little, never reaching zero. A live system that recomputes from a different start every day can compute a different volatility each day, even for a trade that closed long ago. A rolling median of the True Range looks only at the last N bars, and history outside the window has no effect on it.

The later clock strategy studies (the weekday × Market Profile combination and its portfolio) replace every volatility measure with a median:

PurposeDaily ATR versionMedian version
Unit of P&L and distancesDaily ATR(72)Median True Range of the previous 143 daily bars, about 0.92 times the daily ATR(72)
SuperTrend bandWilder ATR NMedian of 2N − 1 bars (the same average lag), multiplier scaled by the measured ATR ÷ median ratio: 1.33 on 1 hour, 1.30 on 4 hours, 1.07 daily
Daily volatility levelATR(24)47-bar median, ranked over a 168-day window
Daily volatility changeATR(6) ÷ ATR(24)11-bar median ÷ 47-bar median

A median needs a warm-up. The data starts on November 4, 2021, and the last indicator to complete its warm-up is the daily volatility level: the 47-bar median needs its first value and then a full 168-day rank window, complete on June 16, 2022. Every statistic in the median version therefore starts on June 20, 2022; the earlier data only warms the indicators up. This is one more reason numbers from the two versions do not compare directly.

How do MAE, G_MFE and MFE differ?

Excursions describe how far price moved while a trade was held, measured only from the actual fill to the exit. The examples below all use the same long trade, entered at 100 and exited at 102 on bar 7:

Bar1234567
High100.510310299104106105
Low9910198.59799103101

Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE)

The furthest price moved against the position while it was held. For a long it is entry price minus low; for a short, high minus entry price; it is 0 if there was no adverse move. In the example the low of 97 is on bar 4, so MAE = 3. MAE is the main basis for setting a stop loss.

Global MFE (G_MFE)

The furthest price moved in the position’s favor over the whole holding period. For a long it is high minus entry price. In the example the high of 106 is on bar 6, so G_MFE = 6. G_MFE and MAE are measured independently, and either can come first. G_MFE is the basis for setting take profit and for judging how much profit potential a trade once had.

MFE before MAE (MFE)

Measures only the largest favorable move before MAE occurs (excluding the MAE bar itself); if there is no adverse move, it is measured until exit. In the example MAE is on bar 4, and the highest high of the first three bars is 103, so MFE = 3. Because MFE’s window is part of G_MFE’s window, MFE is never larger than G_MFE.

Most textbooks call the largest favorable move over the whole holding period MFE, which corresponds to G_MFE here. When the two differ a lot, price first moved a little in the favorable direction, pulled back to the MAE, and only then made its main move.

MAE before MFE (MAE_LV1)

The largest adverse move before MFE occurs, that is, the pullback before the first favorable leg. In the example MFE is on bar 2 and bar 1’s low is 99, so MAE_LV1 = 1.

Maximum High-Low (MHL)

The deepest drop from any high to a later low while the position is held; for a short, the highest bounce from a low. In the example, the drop from 103 on bar 2 to 97 on bar 4 gives MHL = 6; the drop from 106 on bar 6 to 101 on bar 7 is only 5. MHL is never smaller than MAE and is a reference for setting trailing stop width. A bar does not show whether its high or its low came first, so MHL conservatively assumes the high came first (the low for a short), which makes it an upper bound on the pullback depth.

The strategy report has no overall ranking; it lays results side by side from five angles: yearly consistency looks at positive years and the worst year, parameter stability at the parameter neighborhood, risk at drawdowns, returns at total return and expectancy, and diversification at correlation.

How is strategy research layered?

A strategy starts from a root strategy and grows one layer at a time; each layer changes only one thing on top of the parameter set picked at the previous layer. The order is the same across the site:

LayerWhat changesQuestion it answers
Root strategySignal, direction and anchor time; enter when the signal appears, exit at a fixed timeDoes the signal itself have an edge?
Delayed entryFavorable or adverse delayed entry, and the entry distanceCan a different entry price take less adverse movement?
Stop loss / take profitStop loss (SL), take profit (TP)Where should the stop and the target go?
BreakevenThe breakeven stop’s trigger and lockOnce the trade moves the right way, can it avoid losing again?
Trailing stopThe trailing stop distanceHow do you follow the move and keep the profit?
FilterMarket-state conditions that decide which days not to tradeAre the blocked days really worse?
PortfolioWhich strategies trade together and how much risk each takesHow is risk shared when they run together?

The filter comes last and is applied to a finished strategy: it changes which days are traded, and adding it early would make every later layer pick its parameters on a hand-picked set of days. When a study actually ran its layers in a different order, the article describes that order as it was, using the same names; where a number is needed it writes “layer N: delayed entry”. For the full explanation, see Research map and layered method.

Why does a backtest need a control?

A good backtest result can come from factors outside the strategy, such as the entry time or the market’s trend over the whole period. A control removes only the condition being tested and keeps everything else the same, so the difference between the two is the contribution of the condition itself. Our studies use a different control depending on the condition being tested, and always say which one:

Condition testedControlWhat the signal has to do
The signal itselfUnconditional entry at the same time in the same direction, held to the same exit timeBeat the control’s expectancy; comparing only against 0 may just ride that session’s direction
Whether it is a Market Profile effectThe mirror event: the same rule flipped to the other side of valueEvent and mirror both hold under the same hypothesis; if both only make money long (or both only short), the result is the session’s direction
This layer’s settingThe parent: the parameter set picked at the previous layerBeat the parent, so the added complexity of this layer is worth it
A filterCircular-shift null: the same number of blocked days at scrambled timesBeat most shifted versions; see “How do you tell a filter carries information?” below
Market contextBuy and hold, session holdReturns are compared with session hold; buy and hold only gives market context, such as the 2022 bear market

An example of the session control: in volatility-normalized mode, shorting US100 from 06:30–08:30 and holding to 16:00 made a small profit on its own over 2022–2026. An early-session signal that only makes money short may just ride that session’s direction, so a Market Profile signal has to beat unconditional entry at the same time before the signal itself counts as contributing.

When reading a control comparison, note that the signals are the best results picked from many parameter sets while the control is not picked at all, so the gap overstates the signal’s true increment. Beating the control is necessary, not a proven edge.

What do favorable and adverse delayed entry mean?

Delayed entry means not entering as soon as the signal appears, but resting an order and waiting for price to reach a certain level. There are only two kinds, depending on the order:

TermOrderHow a long order is placedPurpose
Immediate entry—Enter at the open of the bar where the signal appearsThe root strategy’s entry
Favorable delayed entryBuy/Sell Limit (limit order)Below the signal price, filled only if price pulls back to itEnter at a better price to reduce MAE
Adverse delayed entryBuy/Sell Stop (stop order)Above the signal price, filled only if price breaks out to itWait for price to confirm direction and filter out failed signals

Shorts are the reverse: favorable delayed entry rests a Sell Limit above the signal price and waits for a bounce, and adverse delayed entry rests a Sell Stop below it and waits for a breakdown. The distance between the order price and the signal price is called the entry distance, expressed as a multiple of volatility. The share of the root strategy’s signals that end up filled is called the fill rate.

The same two methods go by other names elsewhere; they all mean these two, and this site uses only “favorable delayed entry” and “adverse delayed entry”:

Other names for favorable delayed entryOther names for adverse delayed entry
Buy Limit / Sell Limit (order type)Buy Stop / Sell Stop
Pullback entryConfirmation entry, breakout entry
With-trend entryCounter-trend entry
Report parameter value favorableReport parameter value adverse

“With-trend” and “counter-trend” are easily confused with the market’s direction (adverse delayed entry is exactly the one that waits for price to move the position’s way first), so they are not used as names.

Favorable and adverse describe how the order is filled, not whether the position makes or loses money: a limit order fills at a better price than the market when the order was placed, so it is favorable; a stop order fills at a worse price than the market when the order was placed, so it is adverse. Once in the trade, the view switches to the position’s. Take a long with a signal price of 100:

  • Favorable delayed entry is placed at 98 and fills when price drops to 98. If price then keeps falling to 96, that drop is adverse to the position and counts as MAE = 2.
  • Adverse delayed entry is placed at 102 and fills when price rises to 102. If price then keeps rising to 105, that rise is favorable to the position and counts as G_MFE = 3.

The same downward move fills a favorable delayed entry while the order is working, but is an adverse excursion once the position is open. So when reading our articles: favorable and adverse in delayed entry describe only how you enter; the move after the fill is always described with MAE and G_MFE. Exits don’t have this problem: stop loss, trailing stop and breakeven stop are all adverse (protective stops that fill at a price unfavorable to the position), and take profit is favorable, so the order’s view and the position’s view agree.

Why does resting a better price often fail to lower MAE?

Because MAE is close to “memoryless”: once price has already moved against you by some amount, how much further it goes is about the same as right after entry. A favorable delayed entry only fills when price moves against you to the order price, so a filled trade is like entering afresh, and the remaining MAE distribution doesn’t shrink much; the ones that don’t fill are mostly trades that went the right way right after entry.

On 26 US100 Market Profile setups (late January 2022 to September 23, 2026, Tuesday to Friday, MAE and G_MFE in daily ATR), pushing favorable delayed entry out to 0.36 × daily ATR, where only 42% of signals fill, lowers the filled trades’ average MAE by only 11.2% (median of the 26 setups), while G_MFE falls 13.0%; only 12 setups lower MAE more than G_MFE. So a favorable delayed entry cannot be judged by whether MAE falls alone; read it together with the fall in G_MFE and look at which trades did not fill. Adverse delayed entry works the other way: signals that never reach the order price are mostly trades that lose all the way to the exit, so what it filters out is mainly losers.

How should a take profit be judged?

By whether the MAE distribution moves down, not by return. A take profit lets trades that hit the target exit early and avoid giving the gain back: for a trade that reaches the target first and then falls back before the close, MAE keeps counting until the exit without a take profit, while with one it stops at the target price. So to judge a take profit, first check whether the MAE distribution shifts down, then the drawdown; a lower return is an expected cost.

A take profit that clearly shifts the MAE distribution down and makes the drawdown shallower has done its job even with a lower total return; conversely, a take profit picked only for a higher return has mostly just avoided cutting the few days that ran far.

How do you tell a filter carries information?

Compare it with blocking the same number of days at scrambled times. The more days a filter blocks, the smaller the drawdown usually gets, simply because there are fewer trades; blocked days also come in streaks, and blocking a whole stretch of the market changes the drawdown too. So “the drawdown got shallower with the filter” alone cannot tell a filter with information from one that just trades less.

The circular-shift null works like this:

  1. Lay the filter’s blocking schedule over the signal days, for example blocking days 3, 4, 5, 17 and 18.
  2. Shift the whole schedule circularly along the signal days, trying every shift from 5% to 95% of the number of signal days; blocked days that fall off the end wrap around to the start.
  3. Recompute the result for each shifted version. The number of blocked days and the length of each blocked streak stay the same; only which days get blocked is scrambled.
  4. “Beats shifts” is the share of shifted versions whose recovery factor is below the real filter’s. 50% means it is no better than blocking at random times; a filter with real information has to beat most of the shifted versions.

The comparison uses the recovery factor (total return ÷ max drawdown) because a filter changes both return and drawdown, and looking at either alone is biased.

An MAE score cannot pick filters

To pick delayed entries we use a score driven mainly by MAE (MAE weighted twice as heavily as G_MFE), with both MAE and G_MFE divided by a daily ATR of about 60 trading days. That ruler cannot keep up when volatility suddenly expands, so on high-volatility days MAE and G_MFE both grow while their ratio barely changes. On 26 US100 Market Profile setups (immediate entry, January 28, 2022 to September 23, 2026, Tuesday to Friday, MAE and G_MFE in daily ATR):

Blocking high-volatility days lowers MAE and G_MFE in proportion, so the profit potential in R does not improve; but because MAE carries more weight, the score goes up. Using this score to pick the filter and the delayed entry together, 18 of 26 setups chose a filter, 17 of them “blocks only in high volatility”; recomputed with the scale-free G_MFE ÷ MAE, only 10 chose a filter, and the improvement picked on the early period did not carry into the later period.

So each layer uses its own criterion: the delayed entry uses the MAE score, compared with immediate entry on the same signal days; the filter uses the recovery factor and has to beat the circular-shift null.

Strategy codes

The Market Profile signal studies call their 16 events G01–G16. Every event reads the previous complete daily Market Profile (built on New York time, 70% value area, 5-point bins) and is evaluated on 15-minute bars from the anchor time (New York 06:30–10:30) to 14:00: the trade enters on the first bar where the event appears, is held to 16:00, and there is at most one trade a day. Every event is tested both long and short, so “G05 Short 08:30” means the G05 event, short, anchor time 08:30.

A mirror flips the same rule across the Profile: VAL becomes VAH, the profile low becomes the high, “below the POC” becomes “above the POC”. The continuation hypothesis, “price that leaves value keeps going”, expects shorts on the event below value and longs on the mirror above it; the reversion hypothesis, “a false break returns to value”, expects the opposite. In the root strategy study (late January 2022 to September 23, 2026, Tuesday to Friday, volatility-normalized mode), the six kept with priority are G01 Short 07:30, G05 Short 08:30, and the 10:30 longs on G08, G09, G11 and G13 N1; for every signal’s grade and first choice, see Do Market Profile signals work on both sides of value?.

Questions this study answers

What do the terms, units and codes in our research articles mean?

Every term has a fixed name and calculation; for example, MFE measures only the favorable move before MAE, and the largest favorable move over the whole holding period is G_MFE. Returns come in three units: percentage, multiples of the daily ATR and multiples of the median True Range. Market Profile signals are called G01–G16, with their conditions and mirrors in the strategy code table.

By what standard is each layer's method judged?

A signal has to beat unconditional entry at the same time and its own mirror; a delayed entry is judged by how MAE and G_MFE move together and which trades it filters out; a take profit by whether the MAE distribution moves down, with a lower return as the expected cost; a filter by the recovery factor, and it has to beat the circular-shift null. Every layer is compared with its parent, and a good result has to be a plateau rather than an island.

Can numbers from different studies be compared?

Not directly. There is no fixed conversion between percentage and volatility multiples, the median True Range is about 0.92 times the daily ATR(72), and the two versions start their samples at different dates. Check the unit and period under “Method and data” before comparing.

Questions raised along the way

The two kinds of delayed entry go by several names across articles. Which one is used?

The site uses favorable delayed entry (Buy/Sell Limit, waiting for a pullback) and adverse delayed entry (Buy/Sell Stop, waiting for a breakout). Pullback/confirmation entry, with-trend/counter-trend entry and the report parameter values favorable/adverse all mean the same two methods; this post lists them side by side, and articles use only the first pair of names.

Why do some studies replace the Wilder ATR with a median True Range?

Every Wilder ATR value carries the effect of where the calculation started, so a live system recomputing from a different start each day gets a different volatility for the same bar; a rolling median reads only its own window. The cost is a warm-up: the median version’s statistics start on June 20, 2022, and its numbers do not compare directly with the daily ATR version.

Why does resting a better price fail to lower MAE?

Because MAE is close to memoryless: once price has moved against you to the order price, how much further it goes is about the same as right after entry. On 26 Market Profile setups, favorable delayed entry at 0.36 × daily ATR lowers MAE by only 11.2% while G_MFE falls 13.0%, so the two have to be read together.

Why can't an MAE-driven score pick filters?

The daily ATR cannot keep up when volatility suddenly expands, so on high-volatility days MAE and G_MFE grow together and their ratio barely changes; blocking those days raises the MAE score without improving the profit potential. So filters are picked by the recovery factor instead, and they have to beat the circular-shift null.
