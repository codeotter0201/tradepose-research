MAE, G_MFE and profit factor: a quant research glossary

The same concept often goes by several names among traders, and the same term can be calculated differently from one book to the next. It also collects the methods our studies share, such as controls, the choice of volatility unit, and how take profits and filters are judged. All our articles follow the definitions here.

Conclusion

Before reading our research, there are three things you most need to know. First, MFE only measures the favorable move before the Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE) occurs; the largest favorable move over the whole holding period is called G_MFE, which differs from the MFE in most textbooks. Second, returns, drawdowns and MAE come in three units: percentage (%), multiples of the mean daily True Range, and multiples of the median daily True Range, and numbers in different units do not compare directly. Third, there are only two kinds of delayed entry: favorable delayed entry (Buy/Sell Limit: a long rests a limit order below the signal price and waits for a pullback) and adverse delayed entry (Buy/Sell Stop: a long rests a stop order above the signal price and waits for a breakout). Favorable and adverse are named from the order’s point of view; once the position is open, a move in the same direction gets the opposite name.

That is the conclusion. Each section below is the evidence for one claim; follow the sections or the evidence links into the strategy report for the data on every parameter set.

What units are returns and drawdowns in?

The units of returns, drawdowns, per-trade P&L, MAE and similar values are set by the calculation mode (Analyzer mode); win rate, ratios and day counts are not affected. When an article offers two or more modes, you can switch between them in the navigation bar.

Calculation modeHow it is calculatedUnit
PercentageEach trade’s P&L ÷ entry price, then summed%
Volatility-normalizedEach trade’s P&L ÷ volatility at exit, then summed; equivalent to every trade taking the same volatility riskvol (multiples of volatility)
AbsolutePrice points per unit; in US dollars once position size is appliedpts

In every mode, total return is a simple sum: each trade’s result is added directly, ignoring position size and compounding.

Volatility-normalized mode needs a volatility series as its unit. Our studies use two, so returns in our articles come in three units:

UnitEach trade’s P&L divided byUsed in
PercentageEntry priceSwitchable in every article; not the main unit of comparison
Multiples of the mean daily True RangeThe simple mean of the previous 72 New York daily True RangesPopular indicator signals, SuperTrend state trades, market benchmarks
Multiples of the median daily True RangeThe median of the previous 143 New York daily True RangesThe option-expiration short; market benchmarks as a switch

Numbers in the three units do not compare directly:

  • There is no fixed conversion between percentage and volatility multiples. Volatility relative to price changes every year: the Nasdaq’s daily point range at 30,000 is far larger than at 15,000, and calm and volatile years have different ratios. Converted to percentage, the same trades give more weight to volatile periods, which changes which parameter sets look best.
  • The two volatility multiples have different scales. A few wide days lift the mean of the True Range but not its median, so the median usually sits below the mean, and the same “0.5 ×” stands for a shorter distance in the median version.

So before comparing numbers from two articles, check their unit and sample period (listed under “Method and data” at the end of each article).

The same volatility series is sampled at different times for different purposes:

PurposeWhich volatility is used
Per-trade P&L in volatility-normalized modeAt exit (exit volatility)
Stop loss, take profit and delayed entry distancesAt entry (entry volatility)
MAE, G_MFE and MFE in volatility-normalized modeWhen the excursion occurs (event-time volatility)

Intraday strategies enter and exit on the same day, so the daily volatility is the same at all three times. Strategies that hold across days fix the volatility at the entry signal and keep it for the whole trade, so exit and event-time volatility equal the entry volatility and are not distorted by how volatility changed during the trade.

For example, a long that makes 2 points with an entry price of 100 and exit volatility of 40 points is +2% in percentage mode, +0.05 vol in volatility-normalized mode and +2 pts in absolute mode.

How is the volatility unit chosen?

Volatility is the unit shared by P&L, stop loss, take profit and delayed entry. Average True Range (ATR) is the average true range over a period, written as “bar frequency + ATR(bars)”, for example daily ATR(72) or 1-hour ATR(460); in our studies it is a simple mean over a fixed window unless the article says otherwise. We choose the volatility unit by three requirements: the same unit as stops and targets, known before the trade, and the same for every trade on a given day.

  • ATR rather than the standard deviation of returns: ATR is in price points, so it can be written directly as “stop loss 0.5 × ATR”, and the true range includes gaps, so overnight gaps count as volatility.
  • Daily rather than 15-minute: a 15-minute ATR changes within the day with the session, quiet before the open and wild after it, so the unit would depend on the entry time. The daily ATR is taken from the previous completed daily bar: every trade that day uses the same value, known before the open, and a resting order’s price stays fixed from the signal to the exit.
  • A fixed window, declared by the study: a short window follows changes in the volatility state faster, a long one moves less from week to week. On multi-day SuperTrend trades, a 21-day mean kept MAE far steadier across years and volatility levels than a 72-day mean; see Should ATR be weighted by trading session?. The window is part of the study: changing it changes every grid and every result, so it is a new study, not a parameter.

A window of 72 New York daily bars is about 60 trading days: the New York daily series counts Sunday evening’s short session as its own bar, about one bar in six. That bar’s range is only about a third of a weekday’s, so the mean over 72 bars is about 10% below a mean of weekdays only. The offset is steady and does not change rankings, but keep it in mind when converting to points.

Indicators with an ATR of their own, such as the SuperTrend band, count it in their own bars and are written that way, for example “1-hour SuperTrend (ATR 69 × 2.5)”; a number of trading days converts to 1-hour bars at 23 bars a day.

Why a windowed mean or median, not the Wilder ATR?

So that a bar’s volatility is the same no matter where the calculation starts. The Wilder ATR is a recursive average whose every value carries the start of its window: move the start and every later value differs a little, never reaching zero. A live system that recomputes from a different start every day can compute a different volatility each day, even for a trade that closed long ago. A windowed simple mean or median of the True Range looks only at the last N bars, and history outside the window has no effect on it.

The simple mean is the default. A study that wants the unit to ignore single extreme days uses the median instead, as the option-expiration short does with the median of 143 daily bars.

How do MAE, G_MFE and MFE differ?

Excursions describe how far price moved while a trade was held, measured only from the actual fill to the exit. The examples below all use the same long trade, entered at 100 and exited at 102 on bar 7:

Bar1234567
High100.510310299104106105
Low9910198.59799103101

Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE)

The furthest price moved against the position while it was held. For a long it is entry price minus low; for a short, high minus entry price; it is 0 if there was no adverse move. In the example the low of 97 is on bar 4, so MAE = 3. MAE is the main basis for setting a stop loss.

Global MFE (G_MFE)

The furthest price moved in the position’s favor over the whole holding period. For a long it is high minus entry price. In the example the high of 106 is on bar 6, so G_MFE = 6. G_MFE and MAE are measured independently, and either can come first. G_MFE is the basis for setting take profit and for judging how much profit potential a trade once had.

MFE before MAE (MFE)

Measures only the largest favorable move before MAE occurs (excluding the MAE bar itself); if there is no adverse move, it is measured until exit. In the example MAE is on bar 4, and the highest high of the first three bars is 103, so MFE = 3. Because MFE’s window is part of G_MFE’s window, MFE is never larger than G_MFE.

Most textbooks call the largest favorable move over the whole holding period MFE, which corresponds to G_MFE here. When the two differ a lot, price first moved a little in the favorable direction, pulled back to the MAE, and only then made its main move.

MAE before MFE (MAE_LV1)

The largest adverse move before MFE occurs, that is, the pullback before the first favorable leg. In the example MFE is on bar 2 and bar 1’s low is 99, so MAE_LV1 = 1.

Maximum High-Low (MHL)

The deepest drop from any high to a later low while the position is held; for a short, the highest bounce from a low. In the example, the drop from 103 on bar 2 to 97 on bar 4 gives MHL = 6; the drop from 106 on bar 6 to 101 on bar 7 is only 5. MHL is never smaller than MAE and is a reference for setting trailing stop width. A bar does not show whether its high or its low came first, so MHL conservatively assumes the high came first (the low for a short), which makes it an upper bound on the pullback depth.

The strategy report has no overall ranking; it lays results side by side from five angles: yearly consistency looks at positive years and the worst year, parameter stability at the parameter neighborhood, risk at drawdowns, returns at total return and expectancy, and diversification at correlation.

How is strategy research layered?

A strategy starts from a root strategy and grows one layer at a time; each layer changes only one thing on top of the parameter set picked at the previous layer. The order is the same across the site:

Why does a backtest need a control?

A good backtest result can come from factors outside the strategy, such as the entry time or the market’s trend over the whole period. A control removes only the condition being tested and keeps everything else the same, so the difference between the two is the contribution of the condition itself. Every control is an engine run on the candidate’s own recipe, and our studies always say which one they use:

Condition testedControlWhat the candidate has to do
The window itselfThe same trade at other entry times on the same days; noise versions in which all trades of one day flip sign togetherA t above the noise ceiling of its candidate set
Whether a condition adds anythingThe same window on the non-condition days of the same class, for example the other FridaysBeat those days, with the days permuted within their class
Whether the market does itThe same days and direction, held from the entry time to the session closeBeat staying in the market once the information is known
Whether it is a Market Profile effectThe mirror event: the same rule flipped to the other side of valueEvent and mirror both hold under the same hypothesis; if both only make money long (or both only short), the result is the session’s direction
This layer’s settingThe parent: the parameter set picked at the previous layer, on the same entriesLower the parent’s drawdown on a plateau; a lower return is the expected cost
A filterRandom vetoes of the same number of trades, matched on trade sizeKeep a better standardized mean than most random vetoes; see “How do you tell a filter carries information?” below
Market contextThe market benchmark at the candidate’s horizonCompared on return per hour and on drawdown at the same time in the market

For example, the option-expiration short is read against the same hour on all other days and against the same hour on the other Fridays: only the second comparison says that expiration day adds something beyond a Friday morning.

When reading a control comparison, note that the signals are the best results picked from many parameter sets while the control is not picked at all, so the gap overstates the signal’s true increment. Beating the control is necessary, not a proven edge.

What do favorable and adverse delayed entry mean?

Delayed entry means not entering as soon as the signal appears, but resting an order and waiting for price to reach a certain level. There are only two kinds, depending on the order:

TermOrderHow a long order is placedPurpose
Immediate entry—Enter at the open of the bar where the signal appearsThe root strategy’s entry
Favorable delayed entryBuy/Sell Limit (limit order)Below the signal price, filled only if price pulls back to itEnter at a better price to reduce MAE
Adverse delayed entryBuy/Sell Stop (stop order)Above the signal price, filled only if price breaks out to itWait for price to confirm direction and filter out failed signals

Shorts are the reverse: favorable delayed entry rests a Sell Limit above the signal price and waits for a bounce, and adverse delayed entry rests a Sell Stop below it and waits for a breakdown. The distance between the order price and the signal price is called the entry distance, expressed as a multiple of volatility. The share of the root strategy’s signals that end up filled is called the fill rate.

The same two methods go by other names elsewhere; they all mean these two, and this site uses only “favorable delayed entry” and “adverse delayed entry”:

Other names for favorable delayed entryOther names for adverse delayed entry
Buy Limit / Sell Limit (order type)Buy Stop / Sell Stop
Pullback entryConfirmation entry, breakout entry
With-trend entryCounter-trend entry
Report parameter value favorableReport parameter value adverse

“With-trend” and “counter-trend” are easily confused with the market’s direction (adverse delayed entry is exactly the one that waits for price to move the position’s way first), so they are not used as names.

Favorable and adverse describe how the order is filled, not whether the position makes or loses money: a limit order fills at a better price than the market when the order was placed, so it is favorable; a stop order fills at a worse price than the market when the order was placed, so it is adverse. Once in the trade, the view switches to the position’s. Take a long with a signal price of 100:

  • Favorable delayed entry is placed at 98 and fills when price drops to 98. If price then keeps falling to 96, that drop is adverse to the position and counts as MAE = 2.
  • Adverse delayed entry is placed at 102 and fills when price rises to 102. If price then keeps rising to 105, that rise is favorable to the position and counts as G_MFE = 3.

The same downward move fills a favorable delayed entry while the order is working, but is an adverse excursion once the position is open. So when reading our articles: favorable and adverse in delayed entry describe only how you enter; the move after the fill is always described with MAE and G_MFE. Exits don’t have this problem: stop loss, trailing stop and breakeven stop are all adverse (protective stops that fill at a price unfavorable to the position), and take profit is favorable, so the order’s view and the position’s view agree.

Why does resting a better price often fail to lower MAE?

Because MAE is close to “memoryless”: once price has already moved against you by some amount, how much further it goes is about the same as right after entry. A favorable delayed entry only fills when price moves against you to the order price, so a filled trade is like entering afresh, and the remaining MAE distribution doesn’t shrink much; the ones that don’t fill are mostly trades that went the right way right after entry.

So a favorable delayed entry is not judged by the MAE of its filled trades. It is judged on every signal of its parent, an unfilled signal counting as zero, by whether its drawdown falls below the parent’s and below a random skip of the same fill rate. Its total usually falls, because the signals that never pull back include the best ones; one entry opportunity, many exit policies shows how that comparison is built. Adverse delayed entry works the other way: the signals that never reach the order price are the ones that never moved the position’s way, so what it filters out leans toward losers.

When a single bar hits both the stop loss and the take profit, OHLC data can’t show which came first, so the backtest always conservatively assumes the stop loss hit first.

How should a take profit be judged?

By whether the MAE distribution moves down, not by return. A take profit lets trades that hit the target exit early and avoid giving the gain back: for a trade that reaches the target first and then falls back before the close, MAE keeps counting until the exit without a take profit, while with one it stops at the target price. So to judge a take profit, first check whether the MAE distribution shifts down, then the drawdown; a lower return is an expected cost.

A near target shifts the whole MAE distribution down, Q1, Q2 and Q3 together, and usually makes the drawdown shallower while the total falls; a far target is reached by too few trades to move the distribution. Once a stop loss is in place, the stop already cuts the MAE tail, so the take profit only affects trades that gain first and then give it back without touching the stop: it lowers the average MAE and the drawdown, not the tail.

Targets are searched on the parent’s MFE and on its G_MFE as two branches, because they cut different trades: a target on MFE is reached before the worst adverse point, one on G_MFE anywhere in the trade. A take profit that clearly shifts the MAE distribution down and makes the drawdown shallower has done its job even with a lower total return; a take profit picked only for a higher return has mostly just avoided cutting the few days that ran far.

How do you tell a filter carries information?

Compare it with vetoing the same number of trades at random. The more trades a filter blocks, the smaller the drawdown usually gets, simply because there are fewer trades; blocked days also come in streaks; and a volatility state blocks larger trades by construction, since on high-volatility days both the favorable and the adverse moves are bigger. So “the drawdown got shallower with the filter” alone cannot tell a filter with information from one that just trades less or trades smaller.

The random veto null works like this:

  1. Count how many of the parent’s trades the filter blocks and how long its blocked streaks typically run.
  2. Veto the same number of the parent’s trades at random, in blocks of that typical length, matching each blocked trade’s size (the decile of its absolute result), so that the random vetoes remove trades as large as the filter’s.
  3. Recompute the kept trades’ standardized mean (the mean ÷ its standard error) for each of 1,000 draws.
  4. The filter carries information only if its kept trades’ standardized mean beats most of the random vetoes’. A filter in the middle of the draws is no better than blocking at random.

The reading is the standardized mean of the kept trades, not the recovery factor: the recovery factor rises with the length of the sample and moves with the order of the same trades, so it can rank strategies of equal length but cannot test one. A filter enters only as the last layer, on a base signal that has held, with two or three states whose parameters are borrowed from the root or declared before any reading.

How is position size calculated?

TermDefinition
Risk amountThe USD loss of one full stop-out; fixed, it does not grow with profits (no compounding)

Selection inflates every in-sample mean, so the risk amount is set on the in-sample trades with their mean margin cut by at least half: the 95th percentile of their block-bootstrap drawdown, and a day on which every stop is hit, have to stay inside the account limits. These definitions reflect how our research and reports currently calculate each metric, and may differ from metrics with the same name in other books or platforms, so confirm the definition before comparing numbers. Questions this study answers

What do the terms and units in our research articles mean?

Every term has a fixed name and calculation; for example, MFE measures only the favorable move before MAE, and the largest favorable move over the whole holding period is G_MFE. Returns come in three units: percentage, multiples of the mean daily True Range and multiples of the median daily True Range.

By what standard is each layer's method judged?

A window has to beat the same trade at other entry times and the noise ceiling, a condition the non-condition days of its class, and the market from the entry time to the close. A delayed entry is judged on every signal by drawdown, against its parent and a random skip of the same fill rate; a take profit by whether the MAE distribution moves down, with a lower return as the expected cost; a filter by its kept trades’ standardized mean against size-matched random vetoes. Every layer is compared with its parent, and a good result has to be a plateau rather than an island.

Can numbers from different studies be compared?

Not directly. There is no fixed conversion between percentage and volatility multiples, the median of the daily True Range usually sits below its mean, and each study declares its own window and sample. Check the unit and period under “Method and data” before comparing.

Questions raised along the way

The two kinds of delayed entry go by several names across articles. Which one is used?

The site uses favorable delayed entry (Buy/Sell Limit, waiting for a pullback) and adverse delayed entry (Buy/Sell Stop, waiting for a breakout). Pullback/confirmation entry, with-trend/counter-trend entry and the report parameter values favorable/adverse all mean the same two methods; this post lists them side by side, and articles use only the first pair of names.

Why do our studies use a windowed mean or median instead of the Wilder ATR?

Every Wilder ATR value carries the effect of where the calculation started, so a live system recomputing from a different start each day gets a different volatility for the same bar; a windowed simple mean or median reads only its own window. The simple mean is the default; the median ignores single extreme days.

Why does resting a better price fail to lower MAE?

Because MAE is close to memoryless: once price has moved against you to the order price, how much further it goes is about the same as right after entry, and the signals that never pull back include the best ones. So a favorable delayed entry is judged on all of its parent’s signals, by drawdown, not by the MAE of its filled trades.

Why must random vetoes be matched on trade size?

A volatility state blocks larger trades by construction: on high-volatility days both the favorable and the adverse moves are bigger. An unmatched comparison would credit the filter with a shallower drawdown that any removal of large trades produces; matching on size leaves only the information in which days were blocked.
